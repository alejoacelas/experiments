EU carbon futures drop 2.6% as power and steel importers monitor CBAM cost assumptions

Last updated: November 18, 2026 12:15 pm
By J.L.
Share
3 Min Read
SHARE

European carbon futures fell sharply in Wednesday morning trading, giving power traders and energy-intensive industrial buyers another reason to test the sensitivity of their future carbon-border cost assumptions without treating a market quotation as an official CBAM certificate price.

Contents
  • Futures fall sharply
  • CBAM reference pricing
  • Implications for regional trade

Futures fall sharply

The benchmark December 2026 EU emissions allowance future was indicated at EUR 85.11 per tonne of CO2 at approximately 10:31 CET on 18 November, down EUR 2.24, or 2.56%, from the reported EUR 87.35 previous closing reference for 17 November. The new quotation is an intraday value rather than a daily settlement, and the market may move further before trading ends.

The decline follows earlier volatility around European negotiations on the allocation of free emissions allowances to industry. Those discussions remain separate from the price-setting formula for CBAM. Changes in energy demand, fuel prices, investor positioning and emissions-market policy expectations can affect futures prices simultaneously, so Wednesday’s move cannot be attributed to a single regulatory event on the available evidence.

CBAM reference pricing

For EU imports in 2026, the CBAM certificate reference is derived from quarterly EU ETS auction prices; from 2027 it moves to weekly pricing. Neither Wednesday’s intraday futures level nor the reported closing value for Tuesday is the official fourth-quarter certificate price. The latest published official quarterly reference previously discussed in the market was EUR 82.32 per tonne for the third quarter, while the fourth-quarter figure remains to be determined from the relevant auctions.

Implications for regional trade

The difference is commercially important for Serbia and Montenegro, where electricity generators and cross-border traders compare expected EU power-market revenue with emissions-linked costs, transmission charges and available contract structures. Electricity imports are subject to their own CBAM methodologies and actual-emissions eligibility requirements, which cannot be replaced by a simple multiplication of a futures price by megawatt-hours traded. Steel, aluminium and cement contracts likewise need product-specific embedded emissions, free-allocation adjustments, any eligible foreign carbon-price deductions and the correct time period.

For industrial exporters and their EU buyers, actual costs will turn on the legally prescribed reference price and verifiable production data rather than on an isolated intraday move.
