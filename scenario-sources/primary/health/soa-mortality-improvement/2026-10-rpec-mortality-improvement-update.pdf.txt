Executive Summary
This report presents an analysis of recent population mortality experience compiled by the
Retirement Plans Experience Committee (RPEC) of the Society of Actuaries Research Institute
(SOA), with respect to the development of mortality improvement assumptions for measuring
obligations of retirement programs in the United States (U.S.).
RPEC’s 2026 analysis finds that mortality rates for the U.S. population ages 65+ have generally
returned to levels that would have been expected by projecting 2019 mortality rates using MP2021. The ratio of actual (“A”) to expected (“E”) mortality for ages 65+ exceeded 120% at the
height of the COVID-19 pandemic, declined to approximately 101% in 2025, and was 98.7% for
the 12 months from July 2025 through June 2026. The latest result is therefore slightly below the
level projected by MP-2021 and no longer indicates persistent excess mortality.
Longer-term analyses also support the continued use of MP-2021 for ages 65+. After excluding
deaths attributed to COVID-19, estimated annual mortality improvement rates for 2010–2019,
2010–2025, and 2010–2025 excluding 2020–2023 are all approximately 0.5%, which is
comparable to the rate predicted by MP-2021. Therefore, the available data do not provide clear
evidence of a sustained change in the underlying mortality improvement trend predicted by MP2021.
RPEC will not release a new mortality improvement scale in 2026. Although the mortality shock
associated with the pandemic has dissipated, there is not yet enough post-pandemic experience
to develop a new scale using RPEC’s traditional methods. RPEC continues to view MP-2021 as a
reasonable best-estimate mortality improvement scale for retirement plan actuarial valuations,
especially for ages 65+. Actuaries may consider plan-specific adjustments, but RPEC does not
believe that modified assumptions are inherently better predictors of future U.S. population
mortality.
RPEC’s analysis continues to use timely Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) deathcount data and Social Security area population estimates. Because recent population estimates
are subject to revision, the resulting A/E ratios should be viewed as estimates rather than exact
measurements.

Section 1: Potential Timing of Release of an Updated MP-Scale
From 2015 through 2021, RPEC released annual updates to Mortality Improvement Scale MP2014 incorporating the latest available historical mortality data. The most recent scale, MP2021, reflects mortality experience through 2019.
The timing of a new scale depends partly on data availability. RPEC’s MP scales have
historically been developed using Medicare data, which provides internally consistent death and
exposure counts but has a reporting lag of more than two years. To monitor more recent
experience, RPEC uses CDC death counts together with estimated exposures. Section 2 describes
these data sources and their limitations.
Once sufficient post-pandemic experience is available, RPEC’s traditional mortality improvement
methodology could be used to develop a new scale. This methodology applies third-order
Whittaker-Henderson graduation to smooth experience across multiple years and uses a twoyear step-back to mitigate edge effects. As a result, the data used in the model would need to
include at least five years of post-pandemic experience. If the post-pandemic period is
considered to begin in 2024, five years of experience would extend through 2028. Medicare data
for 2028 would not become available from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services
(CMS) until 2030. Accordingly, an updated MP scale developed using RPEC’s traditional
methodology would likely not be available before fall 2030.
RPEC will continue to evaluate emerging experience and consider whether alternative methods
could support a meaningful update sooner.1 It is possible that an appropriate alternative
methodology could produce an updated scale using only three or four years of post-pandemic
experience.
Another consideration is the identification of the post-pandemic period. Although the major
effects of the pandemic are commonly considered to have ended in the first quarter of 2022, U.S.
population mortality remained elevated into 2023. Future analysis will therefore consider
whether post-pandemic experience should begin in 2023 or 2024 and whether that choice
materially affects the resulting improvement rates.
Recent U.S. population experience does not currently provide a compelling basis for replacing
MP-2021. For ages 65 and older—the ages most important for typical U.S. pension and retiree
welfare plan obligations—observed mortality is now broadly consistent with mortality obtained
by projecting 2019 base rates using MP-2021. Results for younger ages differ from MP-2021
projections but generally have limited significance for these applications.
RPEC therefore continues to view MP-2021 as a reasonable best-estimate scale for projecting
future mortality improvement at ages 65 and older. RPEC will continue to monitor emerging
experience and evaluate potential methodologies, but additional post-pandemic data is needed
before a replacement scale can be developed.

Such alternative methods might include: (1) assigning zero weight to the pandemic years of data and applying the current
graduation process; (2) filling years 2020 through 2023, inclusive, with expected mortality data based on the MP-2021 scale, and
then performing the graduation while including data for post-pandemic years as it becomes available, and (3) including data from
the pandemic years but with COVID mortality excluded, and then applying the current graduation process.

Section 2: Data Sources
Data sources and methodology remain unchanged relative to RPEC’s 2025 report (SOA 2025),
but the results shown for historical periods have changed slightly, largely due to updated
historical population counts from the Social Security Administration (SSA).

2.1: Data Sources
The Medicare dataset used to produce the MP-scale provides both death counts and exposure
counts tied together via a system of individual-level beneficiary records. The tight relationship
between the death and exposure data is a key reason that RPEC uses Medicare data to produce
the MP-scale. However, the Medicare dataset has a reporting lag of over two years.
Because timely data is essential for the analysis presented in this report, RPEC used death count
data from the CDC’s WONDER database. CDC data has a relatively short reporting lag and
captures deaths across the entire U.S. resident population. However, unlike the Medicare
dataset, exposures associated with CDC death data must be estimated. Possible sources for
exposure data include (1) estimates of the U.S. resident population produced by the Census
Bureau, (2) U.S. resident estimates from the Human Mortality Database (HMD), and (3) the
SSA’s estimates of the Social Security area population (which is a slightly broader population
definition compared to that used by the Census Bureau). For reasons elaborated in Appendix A.3,
RPEC selected the SSA’s population estimates to facilitate the analysis presented in this report.
To compute death rates for this analysis, CDC-reported death counts were divided by the SSA’s
population estimates by sex, age, and year, and the results were then averaged across broad
age groups. The estimated death rates for broad age groups were age-standardized (as
explained in Appendix C) to remove noise caused by gradual shifts in the age/sex structure of
the population.

2.2: Annual Refinements of Historical U.S. Population Estimates
The U.S. population is large and dynamic; consequently, it is challenging to develop estimates of
population counts with a short reporting lag. On an annual basis, the SSA refines and updates its
estimates (both historical and projected) of the Social Security area population (SSAP) and
releases the results as a component of its annual OASDI Trustees Report. It is not uncommon for
previously reported SSAP estimates for recent years to change slightly from one Trustees Report
to the next.
For RPEC’s 2025 report, SSAP data was obtained from the 2025 OASDI Trustees Report (SSA
2025); for the current RPEC report, SSAP data was obtained from the 2026 Trustees Report (SSA
2026a). Across the two Trustees reports, some small shifts occurred to SSAP estimates for the
65+ population. For the period from 2019 through 2024, the estimated 65+ population increased
slightly, while for 2025 it decreased slightly (as shown in Table 1).

Section 3: Recent U.S. Mortality Trends
This section of the report provides RPEC’s analysis of recent U.S. mortality experience, with a
focus on excess mortality and how it has abated over time since the onset of the COVID-19
pandemic. Subsection 3.1 focuses on the age 65+ population; Subsection 3.2 provides results for
10-year age groups; and Subsection 3.3 focuses on COVID-related mortality as opposed to
mortality across all causes of death. We note that the age 65+ population will likely be of
greater interest to pension actuaries, given that mortality among individuals under age 65 has
relatively little impact on pension obligations.

3.1: Results for the 65+ Population
In the late winter/early spring of 2020, the COVID-19 pandemic began to exert a material
impact on U.S. mortality experience. As shown in Figure 1, spikes in mortality for ages 65+
persisted through early 2022, driving up overall average excess mortality. Since that time,
however, mortality rates for ages 65+ have gradually declined, with more recent periods falling
below 2019 levels.

assumption in most retirement plan applications.

Section 4: The Trajectory of Mortality Improvement Rates for Ages 65+
Even in the absence of mortality shocks such as the COVID-19 pandemic, U.S. general population
mortality rates exhibit significant volatility. Seasonality typically leads to a spike during the
winter, but no two seasonal patterns are identical, with the size and/or timing of seasonal spikes
and troughs varying from year to year. When studying mortality trends, seasonal effects can be
neutralized by focusing on annual (rather than monthly) data; however, historical year-over-year
rates of change are themselves volatile – a year of strong improvement may be immediately
followed by a year of weak improvement, for example.
Because mortality data is noisy, and because only two or three years of post-pandemic data are
currently available, identifying a trend in recent data is challenging. All else equal, the longer a
time series, the more useful it is for identifying trends. One approach for constructing a mortality
series that can be analyzed consistently across the pandemic period is to exclude deaths for
which COVID-19 was the underlying cause, as depicted in Figures 10 and 11.

To estimate mortality trends, RPEC applied log-linear regressions to the monthly time series
shown in Figure 10. Calendar-month indicators were included to separate recurring seasonal
patterns from the underlying trend. In effect, the regressions compare mortality across years
after accounting for the fact that mortality is typically higher or lower in particular months. The
estimated trend was converted to an annual mortality improvement rate. Confidence intervals
were calculated using the Newey–West method, which allows mortality results in nearby months
to be correlated.2
Using monthly rather than annual observations preserves information about within-year
mortality patterns while allowing recurring seasonal effects to be accounted for explicitly. Eight
regressions were run, corresponding to the eight overlapping periods shown in Table 2 and
Figure 12. For each regression, the table and figure show the central estimate for the annual
mortality improvement rate and the 95% confidence interval.
For comparison, a parallel set of regressions was run using annual rather than monthly data. In
these analyses, annual aggregation had no material effect on the central estimates but produced
slightly wider confidence intervals.

Mortality improvement rates have been higher in recent years than during the pre-pandemic
period. The key question is whether these higher improvement rates will continue in the mediumand long-term future. As shown in Figure 12, estimates based solely on post-pandemic
experience remain subject to considerable uncertainty because of the relatively short observation
period. Additionally, while deaths attributed to COVID-19 have been excluded from the analysis,
there is some evidence that COVID-19 deaths were underreported during the early pandemic
period and that the pandemic may have affected mortality attributed to other causes (Tausch
2023).
The regressions for 2010–2019, 2010–2025, and 2010–2025 excluding 2020–2023 have relatively
narrow confidence intervals, with point estimates of about 0.5% for the annual rate of
improvement.3 The similarity of these estimates is consistent with the long-term underlying nonCOVID mortality improvement trend remaining broadly stable when measured over these
extended periods. It does not, however, establish that the pandemic had no effect on the
trajectory of mortality improvement. Because these estimation periods overlap substantially and
estimates based on more recent experience are less precise, the available data do not provide
clear evidence either for or against a material change in the underlying trend.
Additional post-pandemic experience will be needed to determine whether the higher
improvement rates suggested by some of the more recent periods represent a sustained change

5.2: Changes to Long-Term Improvement Rates
Adding a load to MP-2021 does not alter the scale’s long-term improvement rates, nor does it
alter the underlying trajectory and speed for transitioning from near-term to long-term rates. An
adjustment to transition speed or the ultimate improvement rates is a deeper, more fundamental
adjustment than adding a mortality load. Such an adjustment is best described as the creation
of a new scale rather than simply a modification of MP-2021. While the MIM workbook offers
this capability, RPEC is of the opinion that there is not yet sufficient post-pandemic data to
develop a new MP scale with greater predictive power for the general U.S. age 65+ population
than MP-2021. Additionally, the A/E analysis presented in Section 3 indicates that MP-2021 is
well-aligned with recent mortality experience for ages 65+.

5.3: Potential Impact of GLP-1 Utilization
There has been some recent attention to the increased utilization of glucagon-like peptide-1
(GLP-1) agonists and its impact on population mortality. U.S. reinsurers have recently published
analyses suggesting that increased GLP-1 use may partly explain recent mortality reductions.
Some reinsurers have gone a step further by estimating how much future mortality improvement
could be attributed specifically to GLP-1 utilization over an extended period.
In the United Kingdom, the Continuous Mortality Investigation (CMI) stated in Working Paper
212 (CMI WP212) that actuaries should consider the future impact of medical advances,
including GLP-1 drugs, when setting long-term mortality improvement rates, but provided no
further guidance. In Germany, there is also interest within the German Actuarial Association in
whether actuaries should consider medical advances, including GLP-1 drugs, and their effect on
mortality when performing valuation, risk management, and product development work.
While the recent prevalence of GLP-1 utilization may well explain some of the mortality
improvements we have observed in recent years, RPEC believes it may be somewhat premature
to adjust a long-term mortality improvement assumption for pension and retiree welfare
valuations based on GLP-1-based mortality studies alone.

Canada
As noted in RPEC’s 2025 report, the Canadian Institute of Actuaries (CIA) published new
mortality improvement research and a new mortality improvement scale (CIA 2024) in April
2024, referred to in this report as “CanMI-2024.” At that time, adoption of the new improvement
scale was not widespread, as many pension actuaries were awaiting an updated Canadian
pensioner base mortality table. On March 11, 2026, the CIA published the 2024 Canadian
Pensioner Mortality Research Project (CPM2024). Earlier Canadian pension mortality work
produced the CPM2014 base tables and CPM-B improvement scale in 2014, followed by the MI2017 improvement scale in 2017. The CPM2024 research was conducted by Ad Res Reinsurance
Services GmbH and supervised by a project oversight group of the CIA’s Research Council.
The CPM2024 study is based primarily on mortality experience from 2011 through 2021 and
includes an extended observation through 2023 for a subset of the data. It contains
approximately 1.6 million individual records, about three times the volume of data underlying
CPM2014. There are several differences between the new and prior studies. CPM2014 included
separate mortality tables for public- and private-sector pension plans, whereas CPM2024
provides a “Combined” table as well as “Light” and “Heavy” tables reflecting pension plans
with lower and higher mortality, respectively. The larger volume of data available for CPM2024
supported this approach and allows the selection of a mortality table to be based more directly
on the mortality experience and characteristics of an individual plan rather than its classification
as a public- or private-sector plan. The study also found that surviving spouses exhibited
distinctly heavier mortality than pensioners and, unlike CPM2014, provides separate mortality
tables for widows and widowers.
The experience period used to develop CPM2024 includes 2020 and 2021, but the researchers did
not simply exclude mortality experience during the COVID-19 pandemic. Instead, they developed
a model that allowed them to distinguish normal seasonal variation and the underlying mortality
improvement trend from temporary spikes in mortality. The analysis identified a significant
mortality spike during the initial wave of the pandemic in April and May 2020, which was
modeled separately so that the associated excess mortality did not affect the underlying

Appendix A. Data Sources
A.1: Death Counts
As explained in Section 2.1, the analysis presented in this report uses CDC death-count data.
This data is available with a short reporting lag, facilitating an examination of recent mortality
trends. The data used in this analysis was downloaded on September 23, 2026. Because CDC
data for recent months is provisional and incomplete, the analysis excludes the three most
recent months—July through September 2026. Reported death counts for June 2026 and earlier
were adjusted upward to estimate final counts. RPEC estimated reporting-completeness rates by
comparing successive CDC datasets downloaded at different times during 2025 and 2026 and
observing how reported counts matured over time. For the age 65+ population, the estimated
completion rates exceeded 99.90% for March 2026 and earlier and were 99.90%, 99.83%, and
99.35% for April, May, and June 2026, respectively. Estimated final death counts were calculated
by dividing the reported counts by the applicable completion rate.

A.2: Exposure Data
To compute crude death rates for the analysis presented in this report, CDC death counts were
divided by “Social Security area population” (SSAP) estimates produced by the Social Security
Administration (SSA) for its 2026 Trustees Report.
An asymmetry exists between the geographical area covered by CDC death data and the area
covered by the SSAP counts. The CDC data captures U.S. residents of the 50 states and the
District of Columbia (DC), while the SSAP data captures a broader population. In addition to the
50 states and DC, the SSAP data captures the following population segments: civilian residents
of Puerto Rico, the Virgin Islands, Guam, American Samoa, and the Northern Mariana Islands;
Federal civilian employees and persons in the U.S. Armed Forces abroad and their dependents;
non-citizens living abroad who are insured for Social Security benefits; and all other U.S. citizens
abroad. As a result of this broader definition, SSAP is about 2% larger than the U.S. resident
population. This asymmetry is material with respect to computing mortality rates, but the
objective of this report is to examine mortality trends rather than to quantify mortality rates.
Trends are unlikely to be affected by the asymmetry.

A.3: Rationale for Using Social Security Data for Exposures
To develop the A/E results presented in this report, estimates of population counts are required
by year, sex, and age. Because the report covers the period through June 2026, population
estimates should ideally extend through that month.
Because the U.S. general population is large and dynamic, it is challenging to develop estimates
with a short reporting lag relative to the present. The closer the data is to the present, the
greater its uncertainty. In addition, the uncertainty is greater at advanced ages because
population counts are smaller and errors in age reporting have a greater impact. These
uncertainties are generally modest at ages 65–74 but become more significant at ages 80+.
For this report, RPEC considered three possible population datasets: (1) estimates of the U.S.
resident population produced by the U.S. Census Bureau, (2) U.S. resident population estimates
produced by HMD, and (3) estimates of SSAP produced by the SSA. Because this report focuses
29

on mortality trends rather than the absolute level of mortality rates, RPEC believes that SSAP’s
slightly broader population definition is not a material concern for this analysis.

presumably negligible as a percent of the underlying population. If one assumes net migration of
zero, then each year the 80+ population should change as follows:

BOY = beginning of year
EOY = end of year
Newly80 = number of people reaching age 80 during the year
Focusing on the period from January 1, 2020, through December 31, 2024, the equation above
can be used to derive the following relationship:
Pop(EOY 2024) - Pop(BOY 2020) = Newly80(2020 to 2024) - Deaths(2020 to 2024)
This equation expresses the following relationship: the cumulative change in the 80+ population
from 2020 to 2024 is equal to the number of persons reaching age 80 during this period minus
the cumulative number of deaths at ages 80+. A useful variant of this equation is as follows:
Implied Deaths(2020 to 2024)
= Newly80(2020 to 2024) – [Pop(EOY 2024) – Pop(BOY 2020)]
“Implied deaths” are the number needed to precisely account for the net change in the
population. The closer implied deaths are to actual deaths, the better the fit between the
exposure and death-count datasets.

Some readers may find these changes in A/E ratios counterintuitive. To understand why they
changed the way they did, recall that the expected death rates in 2019 are set equal to the
actual death rates. By definition, the 2019 A/E ratio is 100.0%. The updated SSAP exposure

and thus the expected death rate – decreased. Because the expected death rate in 2019
decreased, the expected death rates in all future years decreased as well, since they are
determined by applying MP-2021 to the expected 2019 death rate.
If the SSAP exposures in each subsequent year increased by the same 0.39%, then the A/E ratios
in each subsequent year would be relatively unchanged. But the SSAP exposures increased by
different amounts, as shown in Table B-1. The further from 0.39% the exposure increase is for a
given year, the larger the change in A/E ratio, because the actual death rate would reflect that
year’s exposure change whereas the expected would still reflect the 0.39% exposure increase
from 2019. The lower the exposure increase, the higher the increase in A/E ratio. This explains
why in Table B-2 (and Figure 5) there is little change in A/E ratios for 2019-2022, but the A/E
ratios for 2023-2025 increased more significantly. The increase in A/E ratio is the highest in 2025
because the updated SSAP exposure actually decreased.

Appendix C: Age Standardization
The A/E results presented in Section 3 are calculated across broad age groups. For example, the
results in Figures 1 through 5 reflect data for ages 65 and above. Shifts in a population’s age
structure can influence the aggregate death rate computed across an age group. In general, if
the average age shifts upwards (downwards) across time, this will tend to increase (decrease)
the aggregate death rate, even if age-specific mortality rates remain unchanged. This makes it
difficult to interpret trends in unstandardized death rates.

shift in age-specific mortality rates, (2) a downward shift in the age distribution, or (3) a

Appendix D: Expected Death Rates
The A/E values presented in Sections 3.1 and 3.2 are determined as the ratio of two agestandardized death rates. The numerator (the actual death rate) is calculated as described in
Appendix C. The denominator (the expected death rate) is calculated using 2019 death count
data because 2019 was chosen as the base year for RPEC’s A/E analysis. Because 2019 data is
also used for age standardization, the expected death rate calculation in any given year reduces
to the following, as illustrated for the age group running from 60 to 69:

sense, since without a mortality improvement assumption the expected mortality is constant. To
determine expected death rates that include mortality improvement implied by the MP-2021

The mortality improvement factor varies by age and target year, reflecting cumulative MP-2021
improvement from 2019 to the target year y that is captured in the numerator of the A/E
calculation. For example, if the actual period is calendar 2023, then the mortality improvement
factor will reflect cumulative MP-scale improvement across the 2019-to-2023 period. A factor of
95% indicates cumulative mortality improvement of 5%, or a 5% reduction in the mortality rate.
To calculate the expected death rate for a period that straddles adjacent calendar years, the
calculation is similar, except the summation in the numerator is broken into separate months,
and the appropriate mortality improvement factor is applied to each month’s data. For example,
suppose the base period for computing the expected death rate is 2019, and the target period to
be compared against the base period runs from February 2024 to January 2025; thus, eleven
months of the target period fall in 2024, and one month (January) falls in 2025. In this case,
expected deaths would reflect (1) data from February to December of 2019, projected forward
to 2024 and (2) data from January of 2019, projected forward to 2025. Notice that each
calendar month in the target period is always paired with the same calendar month in the base
period, ensuring that the numerator and denominator share the same seasonality. In this
example, February 2024 is matched with February 2019, and the projection period is five years
(2024 minus 2019). Similarly, January 2025 is matched with January 2019, and the projection
period is six years (2025 minus 2019).
To generate sex-specific results, the calculations are performed separately for males and
females. To generate combined results for males and females, the calculations are aggregated
across both sexes.

Appendix F: Theoretical Impact of Historical Data Revisions on MP-2021
The first step in the development of each MP scale is to smooth historical death rate data by
age and calendar year. The smoothed data facilitates the estimation of recent improvement
rates. MP-2021 was developed in 2021 using historical death rate data through 2019. The data
through 2016 was obtained from the Social Security Administration’s 2021 OASDI Trustees
Report and was considered final.6 In contrast, the data for 2017 through 2019 consisted of
preliminary estimates. This raises the following question: if MP-2021 were to be recalculated
today using final data for 2017 through 2019 rather than the preliminary data, would this lead to
a material change in the projection scale? To answer this question, RPEC re-ran the MP scale’s
two-dimensional smoothing process using final death rate data through 2019. The impact was
immaterial for ages 65+ and slight for ages below 65. In RPEC’s view, there is no compelling
reason to release a slightly modified MP-2021 scale reflecting updated historical data through
2019. Rather, RPEC’s focus is on comparing emerging data against that predicted by MP-2021
and evaluating options for developing an updated scale that reflects post-pandemic data.

SSA uses different underlying data sources by age to develop its historical death rates. For ages under 65, death counts are obtained from
the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), a component of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and population exposures
are obtained from the U.S. Census Bureau. For ages 65 and older, both death counts and exposures are derived from Medicare en rollment
records maintained by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services. Thus, although the historical death rates discussed in this appendix were
obtained from the OASDI Trustees Report, the underlying data for ages 65 and older are Medicare data.
