Purpose
This document describes the 2026 individual life insurance historical mortality improvement
(HMI) and future mortality improvement (FMI) recommendations approved by the National
Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) Life Actuarial Task Force (LATF) on a call on
September 17, 2026.
For U.S. statutory reserves under VM20 or AG38 using the 2008 Valuation Basic Table (2008 VBT)
or the 2015 Valuation Basic Table (2015 VBT) other than limited underwriting business, the HMI
and FMI scales discussed herein are applicable.
For all limited underwriting business, the HMI and FMI mortality improvement assumptions are
zero for all years.

Background
As part of the work conducted by the American Academy of Actuaries’ Life Work Group and the
Society of Actuaries Research Institute to develop the 2015 Valuation Basic Table, the Mortality
Improvement Life Working Group (MILWG) (a subgroup of the Mortality and Longevity Oversight
Advisory Council (MLOAC)) was tasked with reviewing recent mortality improvement levels
based on available data for the individual life insurance policyholder population.
Since year-end 2014, the MILWG has been tasked with studying and recommending updates to
mortality improvement scales for use with AG38 and VM20 (specific to the individual life
insurance product lines). Each year, the MILWG presents a recommendation for a set of HMI
factors and, starting in 2022, a set of FMI factors to be used in conjunction with the 2015

2026 Historical Mortality Improvement (HMI) Scale Methodology
The raw, unsmoothed HMI rates are equal to the average of a historical component and a
future-looking component as discussed below.
Historical Component
The historical component is represented by the geometric average of the most recent 10 years
(2013-2023) of annual mortality improvement data implied from individual life insured
population mortality data. The source of the data is NAIC and Kansas mandatory data calls. The
methodology for deriving the mortality improvement rates and the associated analysis tool is
examined in detail in the following SOA report: https://www.soa.org/resources/researchreports/2024/ind-life-mort-tools/.

The recommendation for the historical component of HMI for 2026 reflects the use of the insured
data directly for most adult ages, the exceptions being ages under 20 ages and ages over 80. At
ages under 20 where there is insufficient credible insured data, the U.S. general population data
continues to be used as the basis for the historical component. For ages over 80, HMI rates have
been calculated by grading from age 80 insured mortality improvement levels to zero MI at age
92, then remaining at zero for all ages above 92.

Future-looking Component
The future component continues to be represented by the 20-year geometric average annual
mortality improvement level (for 2026, this covers the period from 2023 to 2043 – beginning at
the end of the historical period of data for insured MI and based on the most recent SSA
Trustees report (2026) intermediate assumption). At this time, there is insufficient data to form
the basis of a future projection of MI results directly from insured data.
The 20-year period was chosen for future component averaging because it results in smoother
patterns by age and calendar year, as well as allowing for greater weight being given to the
long-term average.
The average annual rates calculated as above are then manually smoothed to produce final
scales by sex and age.

2025 Future Mortality Improvement (FMI) Scale Methodology
The calculation of FMI has not changed with the 2026 scale with the exception of rates for
attained ages 22-44 as described below. The starting point for FMI is the 2026 HMI scale. The
FMI rates are calculated as follows:

The FMI rates grade from the 2026 HMI level to a long-term MI assumption that is based
on the arithmetic average of years 10-15 of the SSA 2026 Trustee’s Report intermediate
projected mortality assumption.
The FMI rates grade to the long-term MI assumption level over the first 10 years of the
projection.
The FMI rates then remain level at the long-term rate from 2036-2041 (five years).
The FMI rates then grade to zero at year 20 (2046).

For ages between 26 and 40 where there has been significant mortality deterioration, current
general population trends show a continued improving trend over the most recent years beyond
the end of the insured data period available. Therefore, at these ages, and similar to the 2025
scale, the FMI recommendation assumes a return to zero MI levels in 2028, continuation of zero
MI until 2031, then grading to the long-term MI rate assumption in 2036. FMI rates then remain
level at the long-term assumption until 2041 and then grade to zero in 2046 and later.

Appendix A Development of HMI and FMI Factors – Scale Years 2013-2026
This appendix contains the considerations in developing mortality improvement factors for
application with VM38 and VM20.
• Recent Historical Experience Impact – The desire for a methodology that weights the
impact of recent historical rates of improvement with a longer-term assumption (i.e., SSA
intermediate mortality projections) in determining projected improvement rates. This
approach is (at a high level) consistent with the current U.K. Continuous Mortality
Investigation (“CMI”) projection models, as well as methods commonly used to develop
other insured mortality projection scales, including the SOA Mortality Improvement Model
(https://www.soa.org/resources/research-reports/2023/mortality-improvement-model/).
These methods project rates based on past experience, but trend toward a long-term
assumed average annual improvement level.
• Insured Data – For scale years 2025 and forward, insured data is being used directly in the
determination of HMI rates and, therefore, the resulting FMI rates. Insured data is sourced
from the annual NAIC VM51 mandatory data calls.
• MI Long-Term Rate for developing FMI Scales – The MILWG continues to use general
population mortality as the basis for developing the MI long-term rate used for the
standard FMI scale determination. At this time, it is not possible to develop a projection of
mortality improvement using insurance industry data. The MILWG examined several
sources of general population data, including data from U.S. Vital Statistics, the Human
Mortality Database (HMD), and the SSA. The SSA data was selected as the source for
general population analysis for several reasons, including the fact that it is strongly vetted,
that it may be based on better data regarding age at death for the oldest ages than HMD,
and that it includes projections of future estimated mortality.
• In addition to data sources discussed above, the subgroup also researched and considered
additional factors that could impact future mortality improvement experience, including
the following:
o Recent trends in opioid-related deaths
o GLP-1 impacts
o Lifestyle
o Cancer incidence at younger ages
o Impact of changes in health care – availability and treatment
o Technology advances (medical devices to help disabled and elderly – self-driving
cars)
o Alzheimer’s and Dementia impacts
o Vaccinations
o Climate-related mortality impacts – longer term issue – SOA research
o Synthetics (PFAS) – “forever chemicals”
o Microplastics

Appendix B Considerations for Impact of COVID-19 – Scale Years 2022-2026
In 2022, the MILWG, as well as other industry groups working on valuation mortality and
mortality improvement rates, considered appropriate methods to reflect the impact of a shock
mortality event like COVID-19. To ensure consistency in thinking about how to reflect the impact
of a shock mortality event, an industry group was formed in January 2022 to discuss and
develop a set of principles to reflect the impacts of COVID-19 on life insurance and annuity
valuation mortality. This group included representatives from the life insurance industry,
American Academy of Actuaries, Society of Actuaries, and NAIC. A key principle agreed upon by
the group was that the initial shock impact of the COVID-19 event should be reflected in
valuation mortality only to the extent it is expected to continue in the future.
In practice, this principle was reflected in the recommendations by implementing a revised
methodology for HMI and FMI for years 2022 through 2026. The revised methodology is outlined
below.
Revised Methodology
For HMI, for 2022 and 2023 scale years, under the principle of not including the initial shock
impact of COVID-19 to develop the HMI or FMI scales, the historical average component was
calculated as the 10-year average from 2010-2020 and from 2011-2021, respectively. However,
for purposes of this calculation, it was assumed that 2020 and 2021 mortality remained at the
same level as 2019. For 2026, the 10-year average calculation returned to the standard
methodology using a geometric average over the period 2013-2023, respectively.
For FMI, for the first three reserve projection years for all ages where HMI rates were negative,
the FMI rates were adjusted to show continued deterioration in mortality, followed by a zero MI
level. After year 3, FMI continues grading to the long-term rate.
