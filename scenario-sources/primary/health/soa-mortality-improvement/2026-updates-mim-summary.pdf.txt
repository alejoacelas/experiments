Overview
The Mortality Improvement Model (MIM) is a framework developed by the MIM Advisory Group
to provide actuaries with a consistent starting point for projecting mortality improvement across
applications and practice areas.
MIM-2026 incorporates newly available mortality data through 2023 for both general and
insured populations, as well as enhancements to the graphing functionality to improve data
visualization. A video tutorial describing the new graphing features is available on the MIM-2026
web page.
This update does not affect the MP-2021 mortality projection scale. As explained in its October
2025 report, the Retirement Plan Experience Committee (RPEC) concluded that post-pandemic
data remains insufficient to support publication of a new MP scale. 1 Accordingly, like the prior
version of MIM, the updated version retains the ability to reproduce MP-2021.
While the MIM-2026 update does not affect the MP scale (which uses historical population
mortality data, including data published by the Social Security Administration, to estimate recent
mortality trends), updates to the NCHS dataset bring its historical trends into closer alignment
with those observed in the SSA dataset, as explained subsequently in this report.
Like prior releases of MIM, the updated version includes two workbooks:
•
•

A retrospective workbook for analyzing historical data.
A prospective workbook (the “MIM Application Tool”) for constructing mortality
projection scales.

In prior MIM releases, the retrospective workbook was called the “MIM Data Analysis Tool,”
while the prospective workbook was called the “MIM Application Tool.” This naming convention
may have implied a closer relationship between the two tools than intended. Although both
workbooks draw from similar data sources, their datasets differ in important ways (described
later in this report). To better reflect this distinction, the retrospective workbook has been
renamed the “Mortality Trends Explorer.”
To summarize, the highlights of the MIM-2026 update are as follows:
•
•
•
•

Incorporates newly available mortality data through 2023.
Leaves MP-2021 unchanged.
Includes enhanced graphing capabilities.
Updated NCHS dataset brings its historical trends into closer alignment with those
observed in the SSA dataset.

Data Updates to the Retrospective Workbook
The retrospective workbook (now referred to as the “Mortality Trends Explorer”) contains three
separate datasets:
•

NCHS dataset: General population mortality data segmented by socioeconomic decile,
constructed using National Center for Health Statistics death counts and U.S. Census
population data.

•

SSA dataset: National-level general population mortality data produced by the Social
Security Administration.

•

MIB/NAIC dataset: Insured population mortality data segmented by policy characteristics
such as face amount, plan type, and risk class.

The 2026 update extends the coverage period for each dataset as outlined in Table 1.

In the retrospective workbook, the SSA data is smoothed by age (via a process performed by the
SSA), while the NCHS and MIB/NAIC datasets are unsmoothed. The retrospective workbook
includes an optional age-smoothing mechanism that users may apply to the raw data.

Data Updates to the Prospective Workbook
The prospective workbook includes only the NCHS and SSA datasets. The MIB/NAIC dataset is
excluded because its shorter time span makes it less suitable for estimating mortality trends.
Users who wish to analyze insured population mortality trends can do so using the retrospective
workbook.
To prepare the NCHS and SSA datasets for use in the prospective workbook, they were
smoothed using two-dimensional Whittaker-Henderson graduation applied across calendar
years and single ages. The smoothed data is used to estimate recent mortality trends, which in
turn serve as the starting point for projecting mortality improvement forward in time.

At present, post-pandemic data is too limited in duration to support reliable trend estimation.
As a result:
•
•

Data from 2020 onward is excluded from the smoothing process.
The final year of smoothed data remains 2019, consistent with prior versions of MIM.

Although the smoothed historical data extends through 2019, MIM begins its projection process
in 2018 by default. As a result, the historical data for 2018 and 2019 is replaced with projected
values. This two-year step-back reduces sensitivity near the boundary of the graduated data.

Impact of the Data Updates on MIM’s “Jumping-off” Point
The jumping-off point refers to the estimated mortality improvement rates in the year
immediately preceding the start of the projection. These estimates determine the starting
position for forward projections. Changes in historical data can affect the jumping-off point and,
in turn, the projected mortality improvements. The SSA updates its mortality estimates annually
by adding new data and revising previously published values. Similarly, MIM’s NCHS data is
updated annually and may include revisions to prior years. In this MIM update, revisions to pre2020 SSA data are minimal and do not materially affect the jumping-off point, but updates to
the NCHS data have a material impact on the jumping-off point, as illustrated in Figure 1.

The prior NCHS improvement rates diverge materially from the SSA rates, particularly above age
80. In contrast, the updated NCHS data aligns more closely with the SSA experience, with
substantially smaller differences at advanced ages. As additional data becomes available over
time, the reliability of NCHS mortality estimates for prior years improves. Therefore, the updated
NCHS dataset provides a more reliable set of estimates than the prior dataset.
The closer alignment between the NCHS and SSA datasets reduces divergence between mortality
improvement estimates derived from NCHS and SSA data, particularly at advanced ages,
resulting in more consistent projection starting points across the two datasets.
Because both NCHS data and SSA data capture the national-level U.S. population, one might
expect these two datasets to exhibit similar or identical mortality trends. It is therefore notable
that differences persist between the two datasets. Identifying the cause(s) of these differences is
worthwhile research, but such an exercise is beyond the scope of this short report. Readers
seeking more information on this issue are encouraged to read “Investigating the Difference in
Mortality Estimates between the Social Security Administration Trustees’ Report and the Human
Mortality Database,” a research paper written in 2018 by Dr. Magali Barbieri, which is relevant
due to similarities in data sources and methodology. 2

About The Society of Actuaries Research Institute
Serving as the research arm of the Society of Actuaries (SOA), the SOA Research Institute
provides objective, data-driven research bringing together tried and true practices and futurefocused approaches to address societal challenges and your business needs. The Institute
provides trusted knowledge, extensive experience and new technologies to help effectively
identify, predict and manage risks.
Representing the thousands of actuaries who help conduct critical research, the SOA Research
Institute provides clarity and solutions on risks and societal challenges. The Institute connects
actuaries, academics, employers, the insurance industry, regulators, research partners,
foundations and research institutions, sponsors and non-governmental organizations, building an
effective network which provides support, knowledge and expertise regarding the management
of risk to benefit the industry and the public.
Managed by experienced actuaries and research experts from a broad range of industries, the
SOA Research Institute creates, funds, develops and distributes research to elevate actuaries as
leaders in measuring and managing risk. These efforts include studies, essay collections,
webcasts, research papers, survey reports, and original research on topics impacting society.
Harnessing its peer-reviewed research, leading-edge technologies, new data tools and
innovative practices, the Institute seeks to understand the underlying causes of risk and the
possible outcomes. The Institute develops objective research spanning a variety of topics with its
strategic research programs: aging and retirement; actuarial innovation and technology;
mortality and longevity; diversity, equity and inclusion; health care cost trends; and catastrophe
and climate risk. The Institute has a large volume of topical research available, including an
expanding collection of international and market-specific research, experience studies, models
and timely research.
