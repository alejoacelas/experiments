INTRODUCTION

Introduction
Employer‑sponsored insurance covers 157 million people under age 65. To provide a current snapshot of
employer‑sponsored health benefits, KFF conducts an annual survey of private and non‑federal public
employers with 10 or more workers. This is the 28th Employer Health Benefits Survey (EHBS) and reflects
employer‑sponsored health benefits in 2026. As in years past, the survey examines trends in employer coverage,
including premiums, employee contributions, cost sharing, offer rates, and employer practices. This year, we
asked employers detailed questions about satisfaction with their health plans; coverage of GLP‑1 agonists and
IVF; actions taken in response to projected cost increases; and firms’ ability to obtain information about health
care costs and utilization. The 2026 survey includes 1,937 interviews with non‑federal public and private firms.
The survey was fielded from December 2025 through July 2026.

Key Findings
• Family premiums reach $28,000. The average annual premium for family coverage reached $28,303 this
year, 5 percent higher than last year. Over the last five years, family premiums grew 28%, compared with
inflation of 24.4% and wage growth of 26.9%.
• Coverage rates remain stable. Among workers in all firms, including those that offer and those that do
not offer health benefits, 55% are covered by health benefits offered by their employer, similar to the
percentage last year (55%). Sixty‑six percent of small firms and 98% of large firms offer health benefits to at
least some workers, with an overall offer rate of 67%.
• Some large firms responded to projected cost increases by increasing workers’ premium
contributions or cost sharing. Among large firms offering health benefits, 44% increased the share of
premiums paid by workers, and 21% increased deductibles or other cost sharing for most services. In 2026,
covered workers contribute an average of 18% of the premium for single coverage and 27% for family
coverage.
• Average deductible nears $2,000. Among workers who face an annual deductible for single coverage, the
average this year stands at $1,955, compared to $1,886 in 2025. Covered workers at small firms face much
larger average deductibles than workers at larger firms ($2,624 vs. $1,772).
• Coverage of GLP‑1 drugs for weight loss has remained relatively stable. Among firms with 200 or more
workers, the share covering GLP‑1 agonists for weight loss is similar to the previous two years. Among
firms with 200 or more workers that cover GLP‑1 agonists for weight loss, 29% say that covering these
medications has had a significant impact on their plan’s prescription drug spending, including 61% of firms
with 5,000 or more workers.
• In‑vitro fertilization coverage. About a quarter (27%) of large firms provide coverage for in‑vitro
fertilization services, unchanged from 2024.
• Mail‑order prescription drug alternatives. A third (33%) of employers with at least 50 workers offer
enrollees access to lower‑cost mail‑order generic drugs through venders such as GoodRx, Single‑Care, or
Mark Cuban Cost Plus. In addition, 9% participate in an international prescription drug purchasing service,
such as ScriptSourcing or CanaRx, to access discounted maintenance medications from outside the U.S.
• On‑site and near‑site clinics. A quarter (24%) of offering employers with at least 50 workers provide
health services by contracting with clinics either on site or near their site. These clinics treat both
work‑related and non‑work‑related conditions.

Summary of Findings
Employer‑sponsored insurance covers almost 157 million people under age 651 . To provide a current snapshot
of employer‑sponsored health benefits, KFF conducts an annual survey of private and non‑federal public
employers with ten or more workers. This is the 28th Employer Health Benefits Survey (EHBS), reflecting
employer‑sponsored health benefits in 2026.

HEALTH INSURANCE PREMIUMS AND WORKER CONTRIBUTIONS
The average annual premiums for employer‑sponsored health insurance in 2026 are $9,804 for single coverage
and $28,303 for family coverage. Over the last year, the average single premium increased by 5%, and the average
family premium increased by 5%. By comparison, workers’ wages increased by 3.7%, while inflation was 2.7%.2 3
Over the last five years, the average premium for family coverage has increased by 28%, compared with a 26.9%
increase in workers’ wages and inflation of 24.4% [Figure A, Figure B].
The average premium for smaller firms (firms with 10 to 199 workers) is similar to the average premium at larger
firms for covered workers with single coverage ($9,593 and $9,865) but lower for covered workers with family
coverage ($27,288 vs. $28,592) [Figure C]. The average premiums for covered workers in HSA‑qualified plans are
lower than the overall average premiums for both single coverage ($8,989) and family coverage ($26,450). The
average premiums for covered workers enrolled in PPOs are higher than the overall average premiums for both
single ($10,272) and family coverage ($29,522).
Premiums also vary with firm characteristics. Average premiums for covered workers are relatively high in the
Northeast for both single coverage ($10,806) and for family coverage ($31,378) and are relatively low in the South
for both single coverage ($9,272) and for family coverage ($26,702). The average premiums for covered workers
at firms with relatively large shares of higher‑wage workers (where at least 35% of workers earn $82,000 a year or
more) are higher than the average premiums for covered workers at firms with smaller shares of higher‑wage
workers for family ($29,139 vs. $27,664) coverage. The average premium for family coverage for covered workers
at firms with a relatively large share of older workers (where at least 35% of the workers are age 50 or older) is
higher than the average premium for covered workers at firms with a smaller share of older workers ($28,689
vs. $27,889). The average premium for single coverage for covered workers in private, for‑profit firms is relatively
low ($9,291); the average premiums for covered workers in private, not‑for‑profit firms are relatively high for both
single ($10,927) and family ($30,783) coverage [Figure C].

Most covered workers contribute to the cost of the premium directly. On average, covered workers contribute
18% of the premium for single coverage and 27% of the premium for family coverage, similar to the percentages
contributed in 2025. The average contribution rates for single coverage are similar for covered workers in smaller
firms and larger firms (16% vs. 18%) but the average contribution rate for family coverage is much higher for
covered workers in smaller firms than for those in larger firms (37% vs. 25%). On average, covered workers
at private, for‑profit firms have a relatively high premium contribution rate for single coverage (20%). The
average contribution rate for family coverage is higher for covered workers in firms with relatively large shares of
lower‑wage workers (where at least 35% earn $38,000 or less annually) than those in firms with smaller shares of
lower‑wage workers (31% vs. 27%).
Twenty‑nine percent of covered workers at smaller firms are enrolled in a plan where the employer pays the
entire premium for single coverage, compared with only 5% of covered workers at larger firms. In contrast, 32%
of covered workers at smaller firms are in a plan where they must contribute more than half of the premium for
family coverage, compared to 6% of covered workers at larger firms [Figure D].
The average annual contribution amounts for covered workers are $1,672 for single coverage and $7,471 for
family coverage, both higher than last year. The average contribution amount for family coverage for covered
workers at smaller firms ($9,687) is higher than the amount for covered workers at larger firms ($6,840) [Figure
C]. Thirteen percent of covered workers, including 31% of covered workers at smaller firms, are in a plan with a
worker contribution of $12,000 or more for family coverage. The average contribution amount for family coverage
for covered workers enrolled in HSA‑qualified plans is lower than the overall average contribution amount for
covered workers ($6,211 vs. $7,471).

REACTIONS TO HIGHER PROJECTED COSTS
Employers have multiple avenues by which they can respond to higher health care costs. These include
increasing the amount of the premium that workers pay for coverage, changing provider networks, or modifying

benefits. The cost of health benefits was projected to increase substantially in 2026, and early projections for
2027 point to further cost growth. Firms offering health benefits were asked whether they took certain actions in
response to projected cost increases for their plan with the largest enrollment.
The most commonly reported employer action by large firms was increasing the share of the premium that
workers contribute toward their coverage (44%). Other actions by large firms included increasing deductibles

and 4% increased deductibles or other cost sharing for most services.

PLAN ENROLLMENT
Health plans are often categorized into plan types based on their coverage of out‑of‑network services and
whether they require primary care referrals. This survey classifies plans into five plan types. Beginning in 2026,
this survey changed how it classifies high‑deductible health plans associated with a Health Reimbursement
Arrangement (HRA). In previous years, these plans were grouped with HSA‑qualified plans in a broader HDHP/SO
(high‑deductible health plan with a savings option) category. HRA plans are now classified as PPOs, POS plans, or
HMOs based on their plan characteristics. HSA‑qualified plans are now reported as a separate plan type.
PPOs continue to be the most common plan type in 2026. Fifty percent of covered workers are enrolled in a PPO,
29% are enrolled in an HSA‑qualified plan, 12% are enrolled in an HMO, 8% are enrolled in a POS plan, and less

SELF‑FUNDING
Many firms, particularly larger firms, have self‑funded health plans, which means that they pay for health services
for enrollees directly from their own funds, rather than through the purchase of health insurance. Seventy‑one
percent of covered workers, including 25% of covered workers at smaller firms and 85% at larger firms, are
enrolled in plans that are self‑funded.
Thirty‑eight percent of covered workers in smaller firms are covered by a level‑funded plan, similar to the
percentage in 2025. Level‑funded arrangements combine a relatively small self‑funded component with stop‑loss
insurance, which limits the employer’s liability and transfers a substantial share of risk to insurers. These plans
have the potential to meaningfully affect competition in the small group market because, unlike insured plans,
they may use health status in rating and underwriting. Level‑funded plans are also not required to provide all of
the essential health benefits that are mandatory for insured plans.

EMPLOYEE COST SHARING
Eighty‑eight percent of workers with single coverage have a general annual deductible that must be met before
most services are paid for by the plan, the same as the percentage last year (88%).
Among covered workers in a plan with a general annual deductible in 2026, the average deductible amount is
$1,955, similar to the amount last year. The average deductible for covered workers at smaller firms ($2,624) is
higher than the average deductible at larger firms ($1,772). For covered workers in a plan with a general annual
deductible, the average deductible for single coverage has increased 18% over the last five years and 34% over
the last 10 years.
Thirty‑eight percent of covered workers in 2026 are in a plan with a general annual deductible of $2,000 or more
for single coverage, similar to the percentage (34%) last year. Over half (55%) of covered workers in smaller firms
are in such a plan, compared with 33% of covered workers in larger firms [Figure E]. The share of covered workers
in a plan with a general annual deductible of $2,000 or more for single coverage has increased 31% over the last
five years and 63% over the last ten years.

Some workers in health plans with high deductibles also receive contributions to savings accounts from their
employers, which can be used to reduce cost sharing liability. Among covered workers in an HSA‑qualified health
plan, 3% receive an account contribution for single coverage that is greater than or equal to their deductible
amount, and an additional 10% of covered workers in an HSA‑qualified plan receive account contributions that, if
applied to their deductible, would reduce their personal annual liability to less than $1,000. Some workers also
have access to funds in health reimbursement arrangements (HRAs) established by their employers, which can
often be used for cost sharing. As of 2026, this survey no longer collects the amounts contributed to HRAs, so the
impacts of these contributions on worker cost sharing cannot be calculated. In 2026, 8% of covered workers are
in a plan that is offered along with a health reimbursement arrangement. Among these covered workers, 61% are
in a plan where the HRA contribution can be used to reduce plan cost sharing liability.
In addition to any general annual deductible they may have, most covered workers also pay a portion of the
cost of care when they use health care services, typically a copayment (a fixed dollar amount) or coinsurance
(a percentage of the covered amount). For physician office visits, the average copayment for a primary care
visit is $27 and the average copayment for a visit to a specialist is $46. Each of these amounts is similar to the
amount last year. The average coinsurance rate is 20% for both primary care and specialist visits, similar to the
percentages last year.

DISCUSSION
Despite early projections pointing toward higher costs in 2026, average premiums increased at a relatively
modest pace. Over the last five years, family health insurance premiums have grown at about the same rate as
general inflation and workers’ wages (28% vs 24.4% and 26.9%).
Heading into 2027, forecasts are again projecting premium increases approaching double digits. How and when
higher health care spending projections translates into higher premiums remains to be seen, but employers
may face increasing pressure to maintain the affordability of their plans if steep increases materialize. In 2026,
18% of employers, including 66% of employers with 5,000 or more employees, said they increased the share of
premiums paid by workers in response to anticipated premium increases. After decades in which the average
share of the premium paid by workers has remained relatively stable, a shift toward higher worker contributions
could raise new affordability concerns.
Much of the policy response to rising health care costs has focused on increasing price transparency,
strengthening employers’ negotiating position with providers, and giving purchasers more information to
design efficient provider networks. Many employers agree or strongly agree that they have the data they need to
understand key elements of their health plans, and most employers that have sought access to their claims data
reported receiving it. How greater access to data will change employers’ role as health care purchasers will be
an important policy topic over the next several years, particularly because many employers continue to place
a high value on broad provider networks. Eighty‑nine percent of employers described the provider network in
their largest plan as “very broad” or “somewhat broad.” Mental health and substance use disorder services are
one notable exception: only 64% described those networks as broad or very broad. Some large employers are
addressing accessibility challenges for these conditions by adding virtual and in‑person providers.
While rising costs remain a significant challenge for employer plans going forward, employers reported high
KFF / Page 19

SUMMARY OF FINDINGS
levels of satisfaction with several key elements of their plans. Prescription drug costs remain an important area
of focus for managing plan costs. Meaningful shares of large employers continue to cover GLP‑1 drugs when
prescribed primarily for weight loss, but lower prices available through direct‑to‑consumer arrangements,
together with the significant cost of these drugs, may lead employers to continue adapting their coverage. Small
shares of employers are also exploring alternative approaches to purchasing some prescription drugs, including
international sourcing and alternative mail‑order pharmacy arrangements.

Survey Design and Methods
KFF has conducted this annual survey of employer‑sponsored health benefits since 1999. Since 2020, KFF has
employed Davis Research LLC (Davis) to field the survey. From January to July 2026, Davis interviewed business
owners as well as human resource and benefits managers at 1,937 firms.

SURVEY TOPICS
The survey includes questions on the cost of health insurance, offer rates, coverage, eligibility, plan type
enrollment, premium contributions, employee cost sharing, prescription drug coverage, retiree health benefits,
and wellness programs.
Firms that offer health benefits are asked about the attributes of their largest HMO, PPO, POS and HSA plans.
Exclusive provider organizations (EPOs) are grouped with HMOs, and conventional (or indemnity) plans are
grouped with PPOs.
Plan Definitions:
• HMO (Health Maintenance Organization): A plan that does not cover non‑emergency services provided out
of network.
• PPO (Preferred Provider Organization): A plan that allows use of both in‑network and out‑of‑network
providers, with lower cost sharing for in‑network services and no requirement for a primary care referral.
• POS (Point‑of‑Service Plan): A plan with lower cost sharing for in‑network services, but that requires a
primary care gatekeeper for specialist or hospital visits.
• HSA (Health Savings Account): A plan with a deductible of at least $1,650 for single coverage or $3,300 for
family coverage, paired with a health savings account. (See the introduction to Section 8 for more detail on
HSAs and other high deductible health plans with savings options.)
To reduce respondent burden, questions on cost sharing for office visits, hospitalization, outpatient surgery,
and prescription drugs are limited to the firm’s largest plan. Firms offering multiple plan types report premium
contributions and deductibles for their two largest plans. Within each plan type, respondents are asked about
the plan with the highest enrollment.
Firms report attributes of their current plans as of the time of the interview. While the survey fielding begins in
January, many firms have plan years that do not align with the calendar year. In some cases, firms may report
data based on the prior year’s plan. As a result, some reported attributes‑such as HSA deductible thresholds‑may
not align with current regulatory requirements. Additionally, plan decisions may have been made months prior
to the interview.

SAMPLE DESIGN
The sample for the annual KFF Employer Health Benefits Survey includes private firms and nonfederal
government employers with ten or more employees. The universe is defined by the U.S. Census’ 2021 Statistics
of U.S. Businesses (SUSB) for private firms and the 2022 Census of Governments (COG) for non‑federal public
employers. At the time of sample design (December 2025), this data represented the most current information
on the number of public and private firms. The sample size is determined based on the number of firms needed
to achieve a target number of completes across five firm‑size categories and whether the firm was located in
California.

RESPONSE RATE
Response rates are calculated using a CASRO method, which accounts for firm eligibility in the study. The rate
is computed by dividing the number of completes by the sum of refusals and the estimated number of eligible
firms among those with unknown eligibility. The overall response rate is 11% [Figure M.1]. As in prior years, the
response rate for panel firms is higher than for non‑panel firms.
Similar to other employer and household surveys, response rates have declined over time. Since 2017, we have
attempted to increase the number of completes by expanding the number of non‑panel firms in the sample.
While this strategy improves the precision of estimates‑particularly for subgroups‑it tends to reduce the overall
response rate.
Most survey questions are asked only of firms that offer health benefits. A total of 1,668 of the 1,937 firms
responding to the full survey indicated that they offer health benefits.
We asked one question of all firms we contacted by phone, even if they declined to complete the full survey:
“Does your company offer a health insurance program as a benefit to any of your employees?” A total of
3,653 firms responded to this question, including 1,937 full survey respondents and 1,716 firms who responded
to this question only.
These responses are included in the estimates of the percentage of firms offering health benefits presented in
Chapter 2. The response rate for this question is 20.4% [Figure M.1].

WEIGHTING
Because we select firms randomly, it is possible through the use of weights to extrapolate results to national
(as well as firm size, regional, and industry) averages. These weights allow us to present findings based on the
number of workers covered by health plans, the number of workers, and the number of firms. In general, findings
in dollar amounts (such as premiums, worker contributions, and cost sharing) are weighted by covered workers.
Other estimates, such as the offer rate, are weighted by firms.
The employer weight was determined by calculating the firm’s probability of selection. This weight was trimmed
of overly influential weights and calibrated to U.S. Census Bureau’s 2022 Statistics of U.S. Businesses for
firms in the private sector, and the 2022 Census of Governments totals. The worker weight was calculated by
multiplying the employer weight by the number of workers at the firm and then following the same weight
adjustment process described above. The covered‑worker weight and the plan‑specific weights were calculated
by multiplying the percentage of workers enrolled in each of the plan types by the firm’s worker weight. These
weights allow analyses of workers covered by health benefits and of workers in a particular type of health plan.
The trimming procedure follows the following steps: First, we grouped firms into size and offer categories
of observations. Within each strata, we calculated the trimming cut point as the median plus six times the
interquartile range (M + [6 * IQR]). Weight values larger than this cut point are trimmed. In all instances, very few
weight values were trimmed.
To account for design effects, the statistical computing package R version 4.6.1 (2026‑06‑24 ucrt) and the library
“survey” version 4.5 were used to calculate standard errors.

STATISTICAL SIGNIFICANCE AND LIMITATIONS
All statistical tests are performed at the 0.05 confidence level. For figures spanning multiple years, comparisons
are made between each year and the previous year shown, unless otherwise noted. No statistical tests are
conducted for years prior to 1999.
Subgroup comparisons are made against all other firm sizes not included in the specified group. For example,
firms in the Northeast are compared to an aggregate of firms in the Midwest, South, and West. For plan type
comparisons (e.g., average premiums in PPOs), results are tested against the “All Plans” estimate. In some
cases, plan‑specific estimates are also compared to similar estimates for other plan types (e.g., single and family
premiums in HSAs vs. HMO, PPO, and POS plans); such comparisons are noted in the text.
Two statistical tests are used: the t‑test and the Wald test. A small number of observations for certain variables
can result in large variability around point estimates. Readers should be cautious of these when interpreting
year‑to‑year changes, as large shifts may not be statistically significant. Standard errors for selected estimates are
available in a technical supplement at http://ehbs.kff.org.
Due to the complexity of many employer health benefit programs, the survey may not capture all elements of
any given plan. For instance, employers may offer intricate and varying prescription drug benefits, premium
contributions, or wellness incentives. Interviews were conducted with the individual most knowledgeable about
the firm’s health benefits, though some respondents may not have complete information on all aspects of the
plan. While the survey collects data on the number of workers enrolled in coverage, it does not capture the
characteristics of those offered or enrolled in specific plans.

EMPLOYER SURVEY LANDSCAPE
Given the complexity of the private health insurance system, estimating changes in health insurance costs is
challenging and depends on both what is being measured and how it is measured. The KFF Employer Health
Benefits Survey provides a comprehensive snapshot of current‑year premiums and uses methods designed to
produce estimates representative of workers and employers with 10 or more workers.
Actuarial and consulting firms often produce prospective estimates of employer health benefit cost growth.
These forecasts often include assumptions about changes in health care prices, utilization, and the mix of
services. Prospective estimates of cost growth may be higher than the premium increases ultimately observed,
in part because employers can mitigate anticipated cost increases by changing plan designs, provider networks,
vendors, or other aspects of their plans.
By contrast, the KFF Employer Health Benefits Survey measures observed health insurance premiums or
premium equivalents, that is the cost of the plans actually offered to workers in the current year. Other
annual employer surveys, including AHRQ’s MEPS‑Insurance Component and Mercer’s National Survey
of Employer‑Sponsored Health Plans, use probability samples to measure employer health benefit costs.
While these surveys use different methods and cover somewhat different populations, over time they have
documented a similar rate of premium growth.

2026 SURVEY
The 2026 survey includes new questions on substance use disorder services, transparency, and prior
authorization, among other topics. As in previous years, modifications were also made to existing questions to
improve clarity and reflect changes in the health care marketplace.
Previous surveys combined high deductible plans paired with HSAs or HRAs into a single plan type referred to
as high deductible health plans with savings options (“HDHP/SOs”). Over time, the prevalence of HSA‑qualified
plans has grown while that of high deductible plans paired with HRAs has waned. The different types and uses
of HRAs also has made it more difficult to make generalizations about these plans. So, beginning this year, the
survey no longer tracks the HDHP/SOs as a plan type and instead focuses on HSA‑qualified plans as a plan type.
The survey will continue to ask employers whether they offer HRAs and what the HRA funds may be used for, but
will no longer ask about the amounts made available in these arrangements.
Due to data availability changes starting with the 2022 SUSB, we revised the firm size categories used for
weighting. Prior surveys calibrated nationwide totals based on each firm’s employment using the categories
10‑24, 25‑49, 50‑199, 200‑999, 1,000‑4,999, and 5,000+. For this year’s survey, firm sizes were grouped into 10‑19,
20‑99, 100‑499, 500‑999, 1,000‑4,999, and 5,000+ employment categories. When analyzing the impact with the
2021 SUSB, the most recent year with both categorizations available, we did not find any changes to our main
findings weighted separately based on the two distinct sets of firm sizes. We also adjusted how our weighting
accounts for California. Statistics of US Businesses (SUSB) public tables include nationwide counts for firms with
500 or more and 5,000 or more employees, and these tables also contain California statewide counts for firms
with 500 or more employees, but not for California firms with 5,000 or more employees. From special tabulations
received from the US Census Bureau, we know the distribution of the largest firms in California to be stable over
time. Among California firms with at least 500 employees in both the 2016 and 2021 SUSB, the share of firms with
500‑999 employees was 34%, the share of firms with 1,000‑4,999 employees was 44%, and the remaining share of
firms with 5,000+ employees was 22%. We retained this distribution to estimate the weighting cell for firms with
5,000+ employees with any presence in California based on the 2022 count of firms with 500+ employees.

Employers are the principal source of health insurance in the United States, providing health benefits for 157
million nonelderly people.1 Most workers are offered health coverage at work, and most of the workers who are
offered coverage take it. Workers may not be covered by their own employer for several reasons: their employer
may not offer coverage, they may not be eligible for the benefits offered by their firm, they may elect to receive
coverage from another source (such as through their spouse’s employer), or they may decline the offer from
their firm. In 2026, 59% of workers in firms offering health benefits are covered by their own firm, similar to the
percentages last year, five years ago, and ten years ago.

• Some workers employed at firms that offer health benefits may not be eligible to participate. Many firms,
for example, do not offer coverage to part‑time or temporary workers. Among workers in firms offering

The survey collects information on the plan with the largest enrollment for each plan type a firm offers. While we

Health plans are often categorized into plan types based on their coverage of out‑of‑network services and
whether they require primary care referrals. This survey classifies plans into five plan types:
• Conventional/Indemnity: A plan with no preferred provider network and the same cost sharing
regardless of physician or hospital.
• HMO (Health Maintenance Organization): A plan that does not cover non‑emergency services provided
by out‑of‑network providers.
• PPO (Preferred Provider Organization): A plan that covers services from both in‑network and
out‑of‑network providers, generally with lower cost sharing for in‑network services, and does not require a
primary care referral for specialist care.
• POS (Point‑of‑Service): A plan that covers services from both in‑network and out‑of‑network providers,
generally with lower cost sharing for in‑network services, but requires a primary care referral for specialist
care.
• HSA‑qualified plan: A high‑deductible health plan that meets federal requirements allowing an enrollee
to contribute to a Health Savings Account (HSA). In 2026, an HSA‑qualified plan must have a deductible of
at least $1,650 for single coverage and $3,300 for family coverage.

SECTION 7. EMPLOYEE COST‑SHARING

Section 7

Employee Cost‑Sharing
In addition to any required premium contributions, most covered workers must pay a share of the cost for the
medical services they use. The most common forms of cost‑sharing are deductibles (an amount that must be
paid before most services are covered by the plan), copayments (fixed dollar amounts), and coinsurance (a
percentage of the charge for services). Some plans combine forms of cost‑sharing, such as requiring coinsurance
for a service up to a maximum amount, or assessing either coinsurance or a copayment for a service, whichever
results in the higher payment. The type and level of cost‑sharing may vary by the type of plan in which the worker
is enrolled. Cost‑sharing may also vary by the type of service, with with different requirements for office visits,
hospitalizations, and prescription drugs.
The cost‑sharing amounts reported here are for covered workers using in‑network services. Plan enrollees
receiving services from out‑of‑network providers often face higher cost‑sharing and may be responsible for
charges that exceed the plan’s allowable amounts. This survey does not allow us to capture all of the complex
cost‑sharing requirements in modern plans, including ancillary services (such as durable medical equipment
or physical therapy) or cost‑sharing arrangements that vary across different settings (such as tiered networks).
Therefore, we do not collect information on all plan provisions and limits that affect the out‑of‑pocket liability of
plan enrollees.

are covered by their health plan. Non‑grandfathered health plans are required to cover some services, such

certain services, such as prescription drugs or hospital admissions, in lieu of or in addition to a general annual
deductible. As discussed below, some plans with a general annual deductible for most services exclude specified
classes of care from the deductible, such as prescriptions or physician office visits.

General annual deductibles for family coverage are structured in two primary ways: (1) an aggregate family
deductible, where the out‑of‑pocket expenses of all family members count against a specified family deductible

SECTION 8. HEALTH PLANS WITH SAVINGS ACCOUNTS

Section 8

Health Plans with Savings Accounts
Health plans can be paired with account options that workers can use to help pay for expenses not covered by
the plan. The two most common account options are health savings accounts (HSAs) and health reimbursement
arrangements (HRAs). HSAs and HRAs both allow workers to pay for health care expenses on a tax‑preferred
basis. Workers enrolled in an HSA‑qualified plan can establish an HSA, into which employers and workers may
make pre‑tax contributions up to specified limits. Employer contributions to HSAs belong to workers once they
are made, and workers may use their HSA funds to pay for health care expenses permitted by law.
In contrast, HRAs are employer‑funded arrangements, and employers can determine how the funds may be
used. For example, an employer could establish an HRA that workers could use only for a specific service, such
as in‑vitro fertilization, or it could establish an HRA that could be used for a broader range of services, or for plan
cost‑sharing. HRAs are nominal accounts, meaning that the employer promises to provide funds if an employee
has eligible expenses, but the funds remain with the employer if the employee does not use them.
These savings arrangements are often (or, in the case of HSAs, always) paired with health plans with high
deductibles. Since 2004, the survey has combined high deductible plans paired with HSAs or HRAs into a single
plan type referred to as high deductible health plans with savings options (“HDHP/SOs”). Since that time, the
prevalence of HSA‑qualified plans has grown while that of high deductible plans paired with HRAs has waned.
The different types and uses of HRAs has also made it more difficult to make generalizations about these plans.
So, beginning this year, the survey will no longer track the HDHP/SOs as a plan type and instead focus on
HSA‑qualified plans as a plan type. The survey will continue to ask employers whether they offer HRAs and what
the HRA funds may be used for, but will no longer ask about the amounts made available in these arrangements.

Health Reimbursement Arrangements (HRAs) are employer‑funded arrangements that reimburse employees
for eligible health care expenses. Employers may make a specified amount of money available through an
HRA for premiums and medical expenses incurred by employees or their dependents. HRAs are accounting
devices, and employers are not required to expend funds until an employee incurs an expense that is eligible
for reimbursement. Unspent HRA funds can usually be carried over to the next year, sometimes subject to
a limit. Employees generally cannot take their HRA balances with them if they leave their job, although an
employer can choose to make the remaining balance available to former employees to pay for health care.
HRAs are often offered along with health plans with high deductibles. In such cases, employees may use HRA
funds to pay eligible health care expenses and then pay out of pocket until the health plan deductible is met.
Certain preventive services or other services, such as prescription drugs, may be covered by the plan before the
employee meets the deductible.
Health Savings Accounts (HSAs) are savings accounts established by individuals to pay for health care expenses.
An individual may establish an HSA if they are covered by an HSA‑qualified plan that meets federal requirements,
including a minimum deductible of $1,650 for single coverage and $3,300 for family coverage in 2026, compared
with $1,600 and $3,200, respectively, in 2025. Employers can facilitate employees’ access to HSAs by offering
an HSA‑qualified plan and may also assist employees by identifying HSA options, facilitating applications, or
negotiating favorable fees from HSA vendors.
Both employers and employees can contribute to an HSA, up to the statutory contribution limit of $4,400 for
single coverage and $8,650 for family coverage in 2026. Employee contributions to an HSA may be made on
a pre‑tax basis, and some employers arrange for employees to fund their HSAs through payroll deductions.
Employers are not required to contribute to HSAs established by their employees, but employer contributions are
not taxable to the employee. Interest and other earnings on amounts in an HSA are not taxable, and withdrawals
used to pay for qualified medical expenses are not taxed. The account is owned by the individual, so employees
retain their HSA balances if they leave their job.

SECTION 10. PLAN FUNDING

Section 10

Plan Funding
Many firms, particularly larger firms, choose to pay for some or all of the health services of their workers directly
from their own funds rather than by purchasing health insurance to cover them. This is called self‑funding.
Both public and private employers can use self‑funding to provide health benefits. Federal law (the Employee
Retirement Income Security Act of 1974, or ERISA) exempts self‑funded plans established by private employers
(but not public employers) from most state insurance laws, including reserve requirements, mandated benefits,
premium taxes, and some consumer protection regulations. In 2026, 71% of covered workers are in a self‑funded
health plan. Some employers who sponsor self‑funded plans purchase stoploss coverage to limit their liabilities.
In recent years, a complex funding option, often called level‑funding, has become more widely available to small
employers. Level‑funded arrangements are nominally self‑funded options that package together a self‑funded

LEVEL‑FUNDED PLANS
In recent years, insurers have been offering health plans that provide a nominally self‑funded option for small
and mid‑sized employers that incorporates stoploss insurance with relatively low attachment points. In these
arrangements, the insurer calculates an expected monthly expense for the employer, which includes a share of
estimated annual claims costs, premiums for the stoploss protection, and an administrative fee. The employer
pays this “level premium” amount, with the possibility of reconciliation between the employer and insurer
at the end of the year, although small employers are often protected from any meaningful additional liability.
These policies are sold as self‑funded plans, so they generally are not subject to state requirements for insured
plans and, for those sold to employers with fewer than 50 employees, are not subject to the rating and benefit
standards in the ACA for small firms.
Due to the complexity of the funding (and regulatory status) of these plans, and because employers often pay a
monthly amount that resembles a premium, respondents may be confused as to whether or not their health plan
is self‑funded or insured. There also may be confusion because different plan administrators (generally insurers)
use different labels to refer to these arrangements. We asked employers with fewer than 200 workers whether or
not they have a level‑funded plan.
• Thirty‑seven percent of firms with 10 to 199 workers that offer health benefits offer a level‑funded plan in
2026, the same percentage as last year [Figure 10.8].
• Thirty‑eight percent of covered workers in firms with 10 to 199 workers are enrolled in a level‑funded plan
in 2026, similar to the percentage last year [Figure 10.6] . Fifty percent of covered workers in firms with 10
to 199 workers are enrolled in either a level‑funded plan or a self‑insured plan, similar to the percentage
last year [Figure 10.7] and [Figure 10.8].

STOPLOSS COVERAGE
Employers purchase insurance, often referred to as “stoploss” coverage, to protect themselves from unexpected
losses for claims incurred by a self‑funded plan. There are different types of stoploss coverage. For example,
a policy may cover any amount that the plan sponsor must pay over a specified amount for each worker or
enrollee (referred to as specific stoploss coverage) or it may limit the total amount the plan sponsor must pay for
all claims in the plan over the plan year (referred to as aggregate stoploss coverage). Stoploss coverage may also
be focused on particular types of claims (e.g., organ transplants). A firm may have more than one type of stoploss
coverage.
• Among covered workers in self‑funded plans at firms with 200 or more workers, 75% of covered workers
are in plans that have stoploss insurance, similar to the percentage last year (75%) [Figure 10.9]. Covered
workers in firms with 5,000 or more workers are less likely than covered workers in smaller firms to be in a
plan with stoploss insurance [Figure 10.10].

GLP‑1 DRUG COVERAGE FOR WEIGHT LOSS
GLP‑1 (glucagon‑like peptide‑1) agonists are used to help control blood sugar levels in people with type 2
diabetes, and have also been shown to be effective treatments for obesity. Common brand names include
Wegovy, Saxenda, and Zepbound. Health plans generally have covered GLP‑1 drugs when prescribed to treat
diabetes, but there is widespread interest in their use for people who have a clinical need to lose weight.
Although the costs of these drugs have been declining, the large number of people who may benefit from them,
combined with the potential for long‑term use, has raised questions about their impact on plan spending. The
increasing availability of direct‑to‑consumer programs for these medications, often at relatively low costs, have
also provided options for employers to potentially fund access through alternate methods, such as account
contributions.
Firms with 200 or more workers that offer health benefits were asked about coverage of GLP‑1 agonists when
used primarily for weight loss in their plan with the largest enrollment.
• Among these firms, 16% of firms with 200 to 999 workers, 29% of firms with 1,000 to 4,999 workers, and
33% of firms with 5,000 or more workers cover GLP‑1 agonists when used primarily for weight loss [Figure
12.12].
– Firms with 200 to 999 workers are more likely than larger firms to say that they do not know whether
their plan covers GLP‑1 agonists for weight loss (19% vs. 8%).
– The overall percentage of firms with 200 or more workers that cover GLP‑1 agonists for weight loss
(18%) is similar to the percentage last year (19%) and two years ago (18%) [Figure 12.13].
– The percentage of firms with 5,000 or more workers that cover GLP‑1 agonists for weight loss is similar
to the percentage last year (33% vs. 43%) [Figure 12.13].

ACCESS TO CLAIMS DATA OF ENROLLEES
Firms with 200 or more workers that offer health benefits were asked about their access to claims data for plan
enrollees. Claims data is defined as detailed information about the services used by plan enrollees, which may
have personal identifiers removed, and the amounts paid to providers for each service.
• Among these firms, 27% say that it is “very easy” to access claims data from their plan administrator or
vendors, 25% say that it is “somewhat easy,” 7% say that it is “somewhat difficult,” 10% say that it is “very
difficult,” 25% say that they have not tried to access claims data, and 5% say that they do not know [Figure
12.24].
• Firms that have been able to access claims data for their enrollees were asked how helpful the data has
been in making decisions about health benefits. Sixty‑six percent of these firms say that their claims data
has been “a great deal” of help, 29% say that it has been “somewhat” helpful, 4% say that it has provided
“very little” help, and 1% say that it has been “not at all” helpful [Figure 12.25].

EMPLOYER ACCESS TO INFORMATION ABOUT THE COST AND USE OF
PRESCRIPTION DRUGS
Firms with 200 or more workers that offer health benefits were asked about their ability to obtain information
about the cost and use of prescription drugs in their plan with the largest enrollment. Specifically, firms were
asked how strongly they agreed or disagreed with statements about their ability to obtain sufficient information
about the following:
• Prescription medications used by plan enrollees. Twenty‑four percent of these firms “strongly agree”
that they are able to get sufficient information about the prescription medications used by plan enrollees,
40% “agree,” 14% “disagree,” 7% “strongly disagree,” and 15% say they do not know [Figure 12.26].
• Rebates and other manufacturer payments related to prescription drug use by plan enrollees.
Fifteen percent of these firms “strongly agree” that they are able to get sufficient information about rebates
and other payments, such as administrative fees and incentives, that drug manufacturers pay to the insurer,
administrator, or PBM related to prescription drug use by plan enrollees, 34% “agree,” 17% “disagree,” 11%
“strongly disagree,” and 23% say they do not know [Figure 12.26].
