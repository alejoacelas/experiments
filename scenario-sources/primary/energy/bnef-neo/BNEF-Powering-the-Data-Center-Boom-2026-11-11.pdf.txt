Data center electricity demand is likely to quadruple by 2035 and account for
7% of global power consumption. Much of this growth is planned for the US and
Europe, where grid-constraints are delaying new connections and holding up
projects. Developers have responded by planning their own gas-fired power
plants so they can reap the huge financial rewards of energizing their facilities
quickly. Renewables have played an extremely limited role in feeding these
needs due to higher land usage and lower dispatchability than gas.

This trend creates two main climate challenges: more emissions-intensive
electricity in the near term and locking in fossil-fuel consumption over the longer
term. Power generation from on-site gas plants is more emissions-intensive
than electricity from the grid in markets where these plants are built.
BloombergNEF estimates that if all announced gas plants to be co-located with
data centers in the US are built and operated, they would boost emissions by an
amount equivalent to 10% of US power sector emissions in 2025. More
importantly, once these projects are online, economics may encourage them to
keep operating well into the future even though many are intended as a
temporary measure until grid links are available. This presents a significant risk
that fossil-fuel use is locked in with further data center development.
Ensuring new data centers can link to the grid is the more sustainable option in
developed markets. Expediting those new connections is key to limiting both the
near-term emissions impact and the risk that fossil fuels are locked in –
especially in the US, where much off-grid gas plant capacity is planned.
Continuing to decarbonize the power system in parallel to ensure data centers
run on the cleanest possible electricity also remains vital.
Climate challenges for data centers vary across markets, reflecting differences
in power-generation mixes, grid conditions and clean-power procurement
options. In emerging markets, the near-term issue is greatest where grid access
is relatively fast but power systems rely heavily on coal. In these places, faster
grid access can increase fossil-fuel generation if cleaner capacity does not
expand alongside demand.
The scale of data-center demand could also create an opportunity: demand
could help bring new clean capacity into the system where policy and
procurement frameworks support the technology.

This BloombergNEF report commissioned by Bloomberg Philanthropies
examines how rapid data-center electricity demand growth is affecting power
systems and how grid constraints are pushing developers toward on-site gas
generation. It sets out a framework of policies that can help accelerate grid
connections and reviews how these approaches are being used in major datacenter markets. The report then examines four very different emerging markets
– Brazil, India, Indonesia and Malaysia – and considers how lessons learned in
more developed data center markets might be applied in the developing country
context. Finally, we examine the implications on grid constraints, fossil fuel
generation and renewable energy deployment in these emerging markets.

Key findings:
•

Most policies to speed grid connections are still too new to evaluate how long it takes
for data centers to get grid connected or what impact they will have on emissions.
Policies analyzed in this report fall into two categories: those that aim to improve the quality of
applications entering the grid-connection queue and those seeking to accelerate processing
of grid mature grid applications. We evaluate four metrics: simplicity, developer burden,
speed and climate impact.

•

Minimum maturity requirements and financial commitments for connection
applications are becoming universal tools for filtering demand queues. Maturity
requirements, which require grid applications to meet basic criteria for eligibility, are relatively
simple to implement and place little burden on credible developers, but there is minimal
evidence whether strict requirements lead to faster connections. Financial commitments
make applicants put capital at risk via application fees, deposits or minimum billing
obligations. These have stronger evidence of success. Ohio Power Co.’s large-load tariff, for
example, reduced its demand pipeline from more than 30 gigawatts to 13 gigawatts.

Flexible connections – where data centers can agree to curtailment in exchange for
speedier connections – could help avoid both off-grid and on-grid fossil fuel
investment. Some data center developers BNEF spoke with in the US indicate they are likely
to respond to curtailment by using back-up diesel generators for low tens to hundreds of
hours per year. While obviously less sustainable than relying on energy storage for flexibili ty,
it would on average be less emissions-intensive than operating a gas plant off -grid and
prevent fossil fuel lock-in risk as diesel engines are not cost efficient to run in the long run.

•

Cluster studies – which have been used previously to assess groups of power generating projects – have not been widely adopted to consider data center grid
applications in batches. Studying large demand connection requests together has the
potential for faster application processing, but this outcome has yet to be proven for data
centers. Previous cluster studies conducted on power projects did not meaningfully expedite
their grid access. Texas’ Batch Zero is the most prominent example of a cluster study for
demand-side connections, but the process has been enormously complex and controversial.

•

Designating zones where data centers receive faster access to energy could be
effective at accelerating data center energization and siting projects near renewable
energy generation. France has identified six fast-track sites totaling 4.4 gigawatts. Supply is
pre-reserved in each location, meaning the sites require little near-term grid upgrades. One
site is already aiming to energize 700 megawatts of demand by 2029. The UK’s AI Growth
Zone policy has been slower to roll out, showing that an impactful growth zone policy is not
simple to design.

This report also examines four emerging data-center markets: Brazil, India, Indonesia and
Malaysia. Lessons learned include:
•

Data center growth comes in power systems with different generation mixes, grid
conditions and approaches to connecting large loads. There is thus no one -size-fits-all
solution to managing grid constraints or avoiding growth of fossil fuel power
generation. Still, experience from major data-center markets can help identify which
approaches are relevant and where planning for grid access and power supply may be
needed.

•

In India, Indonesia and Malaysia, the wider power system needs to shift toward cleaner
energy for grid-connected data centers to avoid increasing fossil-fuel generation. The
extent to which data-center demand can support new renewable capacity in the wider power
system varies by market. It depends on the procurement options available and whether
corporate demand can stimulate new renewables.

•

In India, the main grid challenge is expanding local transmission and substation
capacity fast enough around concentrated data-center hubs. Under BNEF’s Economic
Transition Scenario, much of the additional electricity needed for data centers through the
mid-2030s is supplied by greater use of existing coal plants. From the late 2030s, solar and
wind generation grow as their economics improve, supported by increasing storage
deployment. Data-center operators have several ways to procure clean power, and
companies are already backing new solar and wind projects.
In Indonesia, grid access is straightforward where infrastructure is already available,
but large sites with sufficient grid capacity are becoming more limited. Data center
demand in Indonesia is likely to first drive new coal generation in plants with low utilization
rates and then drive up gas demand. Current rules give data centers fewer options to support
new renewables, as they cannot buy directly from independent generators.

In Malaysia, the main issue is whether generation and network capacity can expand

Brazil already faces both a queue-management problem and has physical transmission
constraints. However, BNEF expects renewables to meet almost all of the generation

absorb some of the clean energy capacity that currently is curtailed.

connect to the grid. Faster grid connections therefore have the potential to reduce the climate
impact of data center growth in markets where most activity has taken place to date.
Connecting data centers to the grid does not eliminate their emissions impact, however. New load
growth on the grid will encourage more utility-scale fossil fuel investment if it cannot be met with
renewables. Grid decarbonization is thus essential to mitigate the climate impact of data centers.
This section considers the climate risk posed by on-site gas generation in the US. Yet this story is
not relevant to every market in the world. In many emerging markets where the power system is
heavily reliant on coal – and there is no available domestic supply of gas – the risk of data center
demand growth is not that off-grid gas will be built, but that grid-connected demand will increase
coal generation and extend the lifetime of thermal assets. For more analysis on the challenges
facing emerging markets, see Section 5.

2.1. Data centers could consume 7% of electricity by 2035
The growth of compute-intensive generative AI technologies with broad economic applications
has fueled an explosion of investment in new data center capacity. In BNEF’s base -case outlook
– which calculates future build as a function of today’s announced project pipeline – power
demand from data centers doubles between 2026 and 2030 and almost quadruples to 2,354
terawatt-hours (TWh) by 2035, equivalent to 7% of total global electricity demand (Figure 2).
While this growth is sizable, it arguably represents a lower bound for capacity growth through the
end of the decade as the announced project pipeline continues to grow quickly.

2.2. Grid delays push data centers toward on-site power

Data centers almost exclusively were powered from direct grid connections until 2025. But the
time to link up to the grid has extended beyond the targets of developers for energizing their
facilities in some major markets, particularly parts of the US. This gap is driving some data center
developers to build their own behind-the-meter gas plants to energize projects more quickly.

Prioritizing speed to power
The world’s largest tech companies are rushing to expand the availability of their AI services,
seeking high returns and answering shareholder pressure to move quickly. Some are willing to
pay exorbitant sums to do so, and independent developers that can build data center facilities and
lease compute capacity to large AI labs see huge economic potential from this dynamic.
Anthropic, for example, is paying xAI $1.25 billion per month for access to more than 300
megawatts (MW) of Nvidia GPU computing capacity. Data center developers are thus prioritizing
projects that can energize as quickly as possible, and an obsessive focus on “speed to power”
has come to characterize the industry.

2.5. Renewables’ potential role in on-site generation
Solar-plus-storage can serve a large share of power demand competitively on the grid, but in
most cases it falls short as a solution for providing around-the-clock electricity for data centers on
its own. Data centers have incredibly high reliability needs, asking power to be available upwards
of 99.9% of year. Solar-plus-storage can meet a significant portion – but not all – of this demand.
In addition, solar projects have large land-use needs, making them impractical in cases where
data centers are close to population centers.

Minimum maturity requirements (MMRs) are criteria that grid connection applicants must meet to
be seriously considered by grid operators or utilities for capacity allocation in the connection
queue. These are among the simplest and most widely adopted poli cies for selecting quality
applications, though their stringency varies by market.
MMRs are a simple way for a grid operator to remove or de-prioritize low-quality or speculative
applications because they increase the cost burden of applying in the first place. A good MMR
policy deters bad-faith, speculative applications without being so strict as to deter good-faith
applications from businesses in real need of electricity.
While all markets have slightly different demand connection processes, Table 5 provides
examples of MMRs across four markets (the UK, Finland, Norway and Texas). This comparison
captures the requirements needed at the point where a connection application becomes more
concrete and is allocated some kind of capacity in the demand queu e. For Texas, we profile both
the MMRs required to qualify as “Studied Load” in the transitional Batch Zero 1 process, and the
rules proposed by the Texas Public Utility Commission for what might be expected past that.

Land rights and permitting progress are common criteria in MMRs
Land rights are the most
common minimum maturity
requirement across
markets

Land rights are the most common MMR across markets. A developer can typically meet a land
rights requirement via direct ownership, a lease or an option on the project site. In Texas and the
UK, the MMR rules also define a duration for the land rights. Whil e in Texas the requirement is to
have the land secured for at least five years past the intended full energization date, in the UK,
expected timelines are much higher, at 20 years past the application date.
Securing permits is not always needed, but evidence of having applied for them is a qualifying
criterion in some markets. Norway is strictest on this, requiring applicants to have full land -use
and zoning approval from the relevant municipality before appl ication submission. Finland has
softer requirements, only asking applications to have started the environmental permit process

Financial commitments and MMRs
Grid operators are increasingly requiring applicants to pay upfront fees or securities on top of
screening for MMRs to deter immature projects and derisk capital invested in grid upgrades,
mainly in the US. These are important tools that intersect with MMRs. For example, in Texas,
developers need to pay financial security equal to $50,000 per megawatt of power requested
from Ercot to qualify for and be studied within the state’s Batch Zero program. However,
provisions for financial commitments often extend beyond the application and connection offer
process through to the lifetime of the contract. They are therefore discussed separately, in
Section 4.3.

Norway and Texas are examples of power markets where strict MMRs were deployed to deter
speculative queue applications. Statnett, the national transmission system operator in Norway,
reported a data center queue of 9.3GW as of August 2026, a third of its peak demand of 25.3GW.
Ercot is facing even worse queues, with more than 494GW large load requests as of September.
Norway has potentially the strictest requirements of any market profiled in this section. As well as
land rights and land-use permits, developers seeking to connect to the transmission system must
provide detailed site designs for their project and evidence that financing has been secured until
the next decision gate in the project. This applies to all requests above 100MW, which is higher
than the capacity thresholds in comparable policies in Texas and th e UK (Table 5). As such, even
though Norway’s policy is the strictest, it would impact a smaller pool of projects than other
markets’ policies.
Over what stages of the project the financial requirements should be required were a point of
contention during Statnett’s consultation process for the policy. The policy originally required
projects to provide evidence that financing for the whole project had been secured before
application. Statnett relaxed the provision after industry feedback emphasized this framework was
unrealistic and would create a chicken and egg problem for developers, as project financing
decisions require power certainty. Now, developers must only secure financing u ntil the next
decision gate of a project – from feasibility studies to front-end engineering studies to final
investment design (FID) – and a plan to secure financing for the rest of the project.
It is too early to evaluate how Statnett’s MMRs will impact Norway’s data center development, as
they have only recently been implemented after a quick development process. Statnett proposed
the initial requirement in April 2026 before incorporating stakeh older feedback and implementing
the rule on July 1, 2026.
Texas currently has MMRs that projects must meet to be eligible for its transitionary Batch Zero
process. These are among the more stringent MMRs observed across markets, requiring land
rights, previously conducted grid studies and payment of a pre -grid study security fee. According
to a September 2026 presentation from Ercot, 194GW of load could be studied in Ercot’s Batch
Zero process. This is more than double Texas’ peak load of 91GW and 22GW more than BNEF’s
forecast data center power demand in 2035 for the entire US. However, th is figure is preliminary
and will be subject to the completion of the verification and audit process. Even if all 194GW is
reviewed under the proposed MMRs, an even higher volume of requests will remain in the queue .
This suggests that even relatively strict MMRs, on their own, may not be enough to bring demand
queues down to system peak levels.

The Office of Gas and Electricity Markets (Ofgem) in the UK has opted for a more relaxed

4.3. Financial commitments: Making loads commit capital to
proceed through the queue
Grid operators are
increasingly requiring
larger study f ees,
securities and other
f inancial commitments

Those seeking to connect to the grid at scale have always been required to provide some form of
fee to cover the cost of a grid connection study. Grid operators are increasingly requiring larger
study fees, securities and other financial commitments from large loads as a condition to apply for
and/or accept grid connection offers.
These financial commitments are intended to:
1.

Deter speculative applications: Network operators require large securities posted when the
application is submitted and when the connection offer is accepted.

2.

Capital recovery: Utilities – particularly in the US – are increasingly asking data centers and
other large loads to commit to prepayments and or minimum monthly billing amounts
regardless of final electricity usage. These can and do deter speculative applications, but
their main function is to recoup utilities’ investments in grid infrastructure to serve that large
load. Recouping these costs is especially important if there’s a chance the demand will not
materialize after the investment has already been made. These provisions are typically only
imposed by a utility at the point where a large load signs a binding electric service agreement
with that utility.

Fees and securities vary wildly by market but have similar end goals
Fees and securities for grid applications are becoming increasingly common around the world to
limit applications from non-viable projects. They can be flat fees per application or $/MW
securities that scale with project size and can be required during the application or after a
connection offer is accepted. If the project does not progress, fees may not be refundable. Pre application fees can be refundable if the application is unsuccessful but must be forfeited if the
grid operator makes a connection offer and the applicant declines or withdraws the project early.

Many data center operators are willing to pay huge securities to secure grid connections. Yet if

capacity is enormous, and thus the security is justified. The opportunity is less so f or a large
high-power EV charging station, for example. The UK is an example of a market that has
explicitly targeted its proposed fees at data centers, while other markets are not specific about
the source of electricity.

US large-load tariffs: Deterring speculators, protecting ratepayers
Utilities in the US want to remove speculative projects from their demand queues, but that is not
their only goal. Imposing financial requirements on data centers – in the form of large-load tariff
schedules – are mainly designed to protect utilities and existing customers from paying for
infrastructure to serve large loads that never materialize. Part of the incentive here is that utilities
can earn returns on new investment. However, they also want to make sure to avoid political and
regulatory backlash if those costs are pushed onto other ratepayers.
Large-load tariffs are specialized electricity rate schedules and service rules that utilities create for
customers using large amounts of power; the threshold for these rates varies by utility. The
financial commitments large loads need to make to utilities when they sign energy supply
agreements are well north of the $50,000/MW fees being charged in Texas. Tariff obligations,
however, typically occur later in the project lifecycle and often accompany a guarantee of power
for the project. As such, it is difficult to gauge if developers would be willing to take this risk earlier
in the process, when power supply volumes and timelines are uncertain. Even so, the monetary
commitments data centers are signing onto under these tariffs can provide insight into the scale of
cash developers are willing to pay when power supply is assured.

Some markets are switching to cluster or batch studies, where groups of connection applications
in a region or under a utility’s jurisdiction are reviewed together, rather than sequentially. While
cluster studies for demand connections are being introduced in response to data centers’ load
growth, cluster study processes have been in place for generators seeking connection for years.
The main goal of a cluster study process is to determine what infrastructure upgrades need to be
implemented for mature projects to connect to the grid.
It is unclear whether running cluster studies to review projects is more efficient and faster than
conducting serial reviews. Cluster studies for generation projects have a history of getting delayed
due to high volumes of applications overwhelming the gri d operators. One cluster study being
delayed and being re-studied numerous times has knock-on effects on future clusters, sometimes
leading to cancellations of new application intake. The best-known cluster study for demand
connections today is Texas’s Batch Zero process. California’s Pacific Gas and Electric Company
(PG&E) cluster study process for demand is arguably more mature, having run two demand
connection studies to date, in 2024 and 2025.

SPP, MISO: Accelerated studies for new load and generation
To deal with a growing number of large-load requests, Southwest Power Pool (SPP) – the
regional transmission organization for the central US – developed the High Impact Large Load
Generation Assessment (Hillga) in 2025. This BYOG policy allows large loads to be studied
alongside nearby generation projects and offers an accelerated path to a connection agreement
in 150 days. The basic concept of the process is that loads that are close to similarly sized
generation on the grid will have little net impact on the grid’s operation and reliability. Connection
agreements can therefore be signed without needing to wait for any significant grid upgrades.
Like in Ireland, the Hillga process requires that the supporting generation be in the local network
area of the load. This is defined as within at least two substations, or one substation if the projects
are connected to the 765kV transmission network. The output of the plant needs to be limited to
the hourly demand forecast of the load unless otherwise directed by SPP.
The Hillga process is designed to accelerate connection for large loads, but only as a temporary

program didn’t account for locational and deliverability requirements for the capacity in question.

Do flexible connection agreements help reduce data-center emissions?
Assessing the emissions impact of flexible connection agreements is complex. The analysis
must account for second-order effects, and the appropriate counterfactual against which to
assess flexible operation is ultimately a matter of judgment. BNEF believes that greater
flexibility is likely to support a cleaner energy system overall, in part by reducing the need for
on-site gas generation.
Shifting compute can increase grid emissions intensity based on renewable
economics
A recent MIT study identified two effects of shifting compute across time. First, temporal
flexibility can improve the economics of renewable projects by increasing electricity demand
during periods of high renewable generation. Second, it can flatten the load profile,
improving the economics and utilization of baseload thermal generation .
The ultimate system -level emissions impact depends on which effects dominate, which
reflects the competitiveness of renewable power versus fossil fuels. The study found that
shifting compute to another time reduced emissions in Ercot but increased them in the midAtlantic region of the US, largely due to the differing economics of renewables in both
regions.
The alternative to flexibility could be new gas and coal investment
Another counterfactual is that developers choose not connect to the grid at all. In that case,
developers may turn to building off-grid gas power. Against this new counterfactual, allowing
data centers to use flexible connection agreements is likely to deliver a better climate
outcome by reducing the incentive for on-site fossil-fuel generation.

PJM offers Interim Resource Adequacy Service as alternative to BYONC
PJM explored a rule that would have allowed loads above 50MW to get grid connections without
securing enough new capacity to serve their peak demand – if they agreed to be curtailed during
system-wide supply shortages. Utilities would have been responsible for choosing which loads to
curtail, so PJM suggested creating a large load registry with all loads and their project details.
PJM filed the proposal with FERC in August 2026. It was rejected in October, deeming the service
outside of its jurisdiction because it involved setting retail electricity rates. One commissioner
pointed out that the plan could result in power market distortion, as non -firm demand connections
would be called on to curtail before load that was enrolled in voluntary demand response. This
would essentially create a false signal that generation capacity was adequ ate to meet demand.
While the policy was rejected in its current form, its proposal and rejection provide guidance for
what a future version of the policy may look like.

Texas’ fast-track flexible connection pathway is tougher on large loads
Ercot’s Provisional Controllable Load Resource (PCLR) pathway is similar to PJM’s IRAS service
but arguably more onerous for data centers. Loads are not compensated for participation in Ercot,
and no specific emergency conditions must be met for the service to be called on. Loads can
choose to be a PCLR, meaning they are guaranteed a portion of requested peak demand, but
any power drawn above that threshold is subject to curtailment. The Batch Zero process, which is
the umbrella policy PCLR falls into, has been approved as of June, but PCLR regulations and
procedures are still under development.
While PJM reserves curtailment for capacity shortfall, PCLR loads in Ercot would essentially be
curtailed at the grid operator’s discretion as part of normal congestion and dispatch services. This
is automatically determined by Ercot’s Security-Constrained Economic Dispatch tool, which
decides which power plants to run while ensuring transmission lines are not overloaded. There
are no defined warnings for when loads are expected to curtail, unlike in PJM, where loads have
10 minutes to reduce power draw. No compensation framework e xists for curtailed loads.

How do we ensure grid reliability when switching from the grid to backup solutions?
Data centers can rapidly change their power draw (ramps of hundreds of megawatts per
second) and near-instantly swap from grid supply to an on-site source. In July 2026, a system
fault in PJM led to 3.8GW of data center loads disconnecting almost automatically, called
“transfer-tripping.” This is the largest load-drop event seen in PJM, and nearly double the
events recorded in July 2024 and February 2025 when about 1.5GW of load disconnected.
An abrupt disappearance of load creates massive imbalances between generation and
demand and results in frequency and voltage swings. This is important because e lectrical
equipment has a range of voltage magnitude and frequency within which it is safe to operate. It
took 10 minutes for the grid to stabilize during the July 2026 incident in PJM. The market is
now considering explicit “ride-through” requirements for data centers and other huge loads,
similar to rules that already apply to generators. Ride-through is the ability for assets to remain
connected to the grid during short-term grid issues instead of switching off immediately at the
earliest signs of disturbances. Similar policies are also being introduced in other markets.
Lack of visibility to voltage ride-through capacity from data centers disconnecting is one of the
key concerns identified by the North American Electric Reliability Corporation (NERC). In the
May 2026 guidelines, NERC recommended the utilities make ride -through requirements a part
of connection contracts. Developers could be required to invest in new equipment to meet the
standards, which could add additional expenses to energize data centers.

Section 5.

Implications for selected emerging
data-center markets
Data-center electricity demand is poised to expand around the globe. The growth will be felt
across power systems with very different power-generation mixes, grid conditions and
approaches to connecting large new loads. These markets face challenges in del ivering this
expansion, some similar and some different. However, lessons learned where build is well
underway can help emerging markets identify appropriate policies to suit their unique
circumstances.
This section examines four potential large data-center markets: Brazil, India, Indonesia and
Malaysia. For each, it provides an overview of the data center industry as it is operating today,
discusses main grid-access and infrastructure challenges, and evaluates the relevance of policies
to manage large-load grid connections examined in previous sections of this report. It also models
the impact of new data center power load on grid-connected power installations and outlines
options available for data centers to buy cleaner power.

The challenge of rising power demand
The four different markets each have different grid constraints. Brazil has both a queuemanagement problem and physical transmission constraints. In India, the main challenge is
expanding local transmission and substation capacity fast enough for concentrated data -center
hubs. Indonesia’s challenges vary considerably by location. Malaysia’s main issue is whether
generation and network capacity can expand quickly enough to meet growing data -center
demand.

Brazil has two main grid-connection challenges. The first is the scale and questionable quality of
projects in the connection queue with the volume seeking access today likely exceeding what will
ultimately materialize. This leaves a challenges for the gri d operator in forecasting load and
distinguishing serious projects from those that are less likely to be built. Uncertainty remains in
the wider project pipeline: 92% of Brazil’s planned data-center capacity remains at the announced
stage.
Brazil’s second challenge is physical. Its transmission system already faces congestion in
renewable-rich regions, and even mature projects depend on sufficient network capacity at
suitable connection points. Where requests exceed available capacity at a g rid location, ONS can
use auctions to allocate. Improving the quality and management of the connection queue
therefore does not remove the underlying infrastructure constraint. Projects will still depend on
whether the grid can support new load in the locations and on the timelines developers require.
Brazil’s new access framework addresses different parts of these challenges. Financial
guarantees are intended to discourage projects from reserving grid capacity without progressing,
while periodic access rounds structure when applications are assessed. Auctions provide a
mechanism to allocate capacity where requests exceed what the network can accommodate.
These measures address queue management and the allocation of scarce capacity, but they do
not expand the underlying transmission system.

India does not have a national data center policy, although the federal government supports data

Grid access and infrastructure
Grid capacity is generally allocated on a first-come, first-served basis. Developers apply for load
and the utility assesses whether the network can accommodate it. Assets serving mainly one
project, such as dedicated feeders, connection bays or substation s, may need to be funded by the
developer. Broader upgrades that strengthen the shared network are funded by the utility and
recovered through regulated network charges. Rules vary by state and connection type, and
works may be built by the utility with developer contributions or, where permitted, by the
developer with utility approval.
Developers have limited visibility into competing applications and available capacity at individual
substations, making connection timelines difficult to predict. There is no strong evidence that
speculative data center applications are materially blocking grid queues in India. The more
immediate issue is large connection requests in already concentrated markets such as
Maharashtra, where utilities may need to expand substations and transmission infrastructure to

5.3. Indonesia
Indonesia’s data-center market is expanding rapidly from a relatively small base, with BNEF
expecting electricity consumption from data centers to rise from 3TWh in 2025 to 10TWh in 2030
and 26TWh by 2035. Development is concentrated in Greater Jakarta and Batam. The power
system is heavily reliant on fossil fuels. Coal and gas supplied a combined 82% of state -owned
utility Perusahaan Listrik Negara’s (PLN) on-grid power in 2025, and current rules do not allow
consumers to buy off-site electricity from third-party generators.
Grid-connection timelines are relatively short in Greater Jakarta, where the Java -Bali system has
substantial generation headroom. Physical network constraints become more important where
projects require new substations or transmission lines. Batam has le ss spare generation capacity.
The main fossil-fuel in risk in Indonesia comes from greater use of existing coal and gas
generation in the near term and potential additional gas capacity over time, rather than
widespread reliance on on-site fossil power. PLN offers clean-power products and captive
generation remains possible, but Indonesia does not yet have a clearly established route for data
centers to support new renewable capacity with demonstrable additionality.

Grid-connection timelines are relatively short. A medium -voltage connection, supporting loads of
up to 30MW, can be completed within six to eight months from initial application to energization if
sufficient network infrastructure is available nearby. After an application, PLN typically spends
about two months discussing the demand schedule, conducting site surveys, preparing designs
and completing technical due diligence. Signing an electricity-supply agreement and paying the
connection fee and deposit are followed by four to six months of construction and energization.
Most new data-center projects are large enough to require high-voltage connections, which
typically take 18-36 months. Timelines approach the upper end of this range when projects
require a new substation or substantial new transmission infrastructure, because land surveys,
permitting and acquisition add time. Industrial estates can shorten the process because high voltage connections and permitted land may already be available, reducing total lead times to as
little as 18 months.
Greater Jakarta sits on the Java-Bali grid, where generation availability is currently high. The
system has a reserve margin of about 45%, which PLN plans to reduce to 34% by 2034. Coal and
gas plants on the Java-Bali grid operated at capacity factors of about 62% and 26%, respectively,
in 2025. This suggests that generation capacity itself is unlikely to be the main near -term
constraint in the country’s largest data-center hub.
Network capacity could be more limiting. PLN has consistently fallen short of its transmissionexpansion targets: in the first half of 2026, its national transmission network grew by 0.6%, or 471
circuit-kilometers, while high-voltage substation capacity rose by 0.3%, or 500 megavolt-amperes.
Fuel supply can also affect reliability, as highlighted by the June 2026 Java blackout due to
disrupted coal deliveries.
Batam has less generation headroom, with a reserve margin of only 16%. PLN Batam offers dualsubstation connections as a premium service with binding service -level agreements and a tariff of
$105/MWh. In areas supplied directly by PLN, arrangements such as dual connections or
exclusive use of a substation transformer are generally negotiated with individual projects.

5.4. Malaysia
Malaysia is currently Southeast Asia’s largest data-center market and its electricity demand from
data centers is set to rise rapidly, from 7TWh in 2025 to 33TWh in 2030 and 45TWh by 2035.
Almost all development is concentrated in Peninsular Malaysia, where 93% of grid power is
generated from coal and gas. This makes the country an important test of how rapid data -center
growth can be accommodated in a fossil-heavy power system.
Malaysia’s main grid challenge is not a large queue of speculative projects: data centers are
screened before they can formally apply for a grid connection, reducing some of the queue -quality
problems seen in other markets. More material constraints are generation and network capacity.
The climate risk therefore comes mainly from how new demand is supplied – through greater use
of existing coal and gas plants in the near term and potentially substantial new gas capacity over
time. At the same time, Peninsular Malaysia allows large consumers to buy renewable electricity
from independent generators. This gives data centers a route to support additional clean -power
capacity and reduce the share of new demand met by fossil fuels.

Malaysia differs from markets where speculative connection queues are the main constraint.
MIDA screens projects before they can formally enter TNB’s connection process, reducing some
of the queue-quality risks that other nations suffer. The more material constraints are physical
generation and network capacity limitations, which are contributing to tighter project approvals
and longer connection timelines. The challenge is less about filtering a large formal connection
queue and more about managing and expanding available power-system capacity as data-center
demand grows.
Several measures are under way to address different parts of these constraints. The government
is procuring new gas-fired capacity and encouraging solar projects to locate near data-center
hubs. It also is improving the bankability of corporate clean-power procurement by offering
