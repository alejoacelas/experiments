Executive Summary
The Metropolitan Transportation Authority (MTA) released its July 2027-2030 Financial Plan on
July 29, 2026, showing continued balance of its $21.9 billion budget in 2026, and a manageable
budget gap in 2027, followed by growing gaps in the out-years fueled by costs growing faster
than revenue. Positives for the agency include stronger-than-anticipated tax revenues in 2026,
close adherence to its saving targets and prudent management of federal relief funding that have
supported short-term balance. However, escalating costs are expected to offset these positives
in the out-years. Continued growth in paid ridership, proactive management of its savings plan
expansion and debt portfolio and the outcome of collective bargaining discussions will be key
factors to monitor as the Authority looks to balance its budget in the coming years without
affecting service.
Ridership revenue remains a critical factor for the MTA’s current fiscal position and the path
forward. Paid ridership continues to recover across most modes, though bus paid ridership has
stalled and remains below projections. The MTA has included two fare increases during the
financial plan period to keep up with rising costs. These rate increases are lower than recent
inflation growth, which has worsened in recent years amid geopolitical conflict.
Reducing bus fare evasion remains one of the most immediate opportunities to improve
operating revenues. The Office of the State Comptroller (OSC) estimates if fare evasion were
reduced by 10 percentage points from 48 percent to 38 percent, bus fare revenues in 2026
would have been as much as $150 million higher. Encouraging paid ridership, including by
working with New York City to ensure wide outreach on its Fair Fares program, is pivotal to
reducing future budget gaps.
Prior to the pandemic, tolls and fares paid for more than 50 percent of the Authority’s operating
expenses. In 2025, these sources paid for 34 percent of the total, highlighting a greater reliance
on economically sensitive taxes and subsidies resulting from the State’s fiscal response to help
shore up MTA finances. The Authority must continue to encourage paying riders to use the
system and strengthen the link between its operating revenues and the services provided.
Taxes and subsidies are expected to continue to grow at a reasonable rate; however an
economic downturn could have a more pronounced impact on the MTA’s finances compared to
prior recessions because of the shift in revenue composition. Recessionary pressure would affect
a wide variety of the taxes that the Authority collects, in addition to certain taxes that are already
currently at risk of missing projections, including real estate and gambling taxes. In addition,
projected tax revenue growth, which is closely pegged to State and City projections, is not
expected to keep pace with overall cost growth.
Cost growth remains the most pronounced driver of out-year budget gaps. Costs are expected to
rise by 3.4 percent annually over the course of the financial plan period. Nondiscretionary
expenses, which include nonpayroll labor costs such as for health and welfare; pensions and
fringe benefits; debt service, energy, and paratransit costs are expected to make up about half of
operating expenses in 2026 and are expected to increase 5 percent annually between 2026 and
3

2030, reaching more than half of operating expenses by that year. This rise is expected despite
effectively level pension payments, related to a reamortization of the New York City Employee
Retirement System’s pension obligations, initiated by the City of New York and approved by the
State. The Plan also assumes a decline in claims costs, due to more proactive claims
management and partly fueled by changes to State legislation. Longer horizon strategies, like
those to address claims costs, must be considered to improve fiscal sustainability at the
Authority.
Costs now also reflect a higher base for the MTA’s bargaining pattern of an approximately net
3.8 percent increase based on the Long Island Rail Road (LIRR) pattern. The MTA then assumes
raises of 2 percent annually, a rate that is higher than New York City’s current pattern assumption
for its municipal workers. Talks have not yet led to an agreement with the Transport Workers
Union (TWU), the Authority’s largest collective bargaining unit, which expired in May 2026. The
number of operations and maintenance workers has declined by more than 1,000 since the
beginning of 2026, an ongoing concern, particularly as overtime rises. The Authority and its
workers should come to an agreement that is not significantly disruptive to the financial plan but
provides wage increases that enable the MTA to retain and recruit workers while sharing gains
that may be generated by enhancements to productivity, including more efficient use of overtime.
While these budget pressures are real, measures to address them are at least partly in the
control of the Authority. Proactive monitoring of revenue collections, continued expansion of
recent cost savings efforts and a negotiated labor settlement should all provide clarity as to how
the MTA will maintain budget balance in the coming years. More uncertain are actions that the
MTA is not party to but is indirectly impacted by, including several actions being undertaken at
the federal level. Ongoing choices by the federal government relating to sustained engagement
in geopolitical conflict and the expansion of the federal tariff regime have affected the cost of
energy and the cost of purchasing goods and services, making delivery of services and repairing
the system costlier. At the same time, Congress looks as though it may reduce transportation
formula funding used for MTA capital expenses despite these rising costs, which may create
pressure to find new funds for capital work.
Despite these more difficult-to-quantify risks, the MTA has identified additional quantifiable risks
that are not reflected in the financial plan, most notably for collective bargaining costs. However,
OSC anticipates higher-than-expected overtime costs would be offset by lower-than-anticipated
payroll costs from lower-than-projected headcount, which are dynamic and interplay with each
other. Overall OSC assumes the MTA will have additional resources of $207 million in 2026,
$86 million in 2027, $38 million in 2029 and $85 million in 2030 and slightly higher costs in 2028.
OSC anticipates recently identified unanticipated resources in 2026 will be carried forward to
help to achieve budget balance in 2027. Adjusted budget gaps would be $517 million in 2028
rising to $812 million when including these OSC-identified risks and offsets.
While the MTA faces several fiscal challenges over the financial plan period, many of the key
drivers are known and manageable through active oversight and its near-term outlook remains
stable. How effectively it navigates cost pressures, ridership recovery, labor negotiations and
external risks will determine the sustainability of its financial position in the years ahead without
compromising service.

MTA Utilization Trends
A continued fiscal recovery is reliant on the return of paid ridership to projected levels across the
MTA’s modes of transportation. The COVID-19 pandemic reached New York City in March 2020,
causing steep drop-offs in MTA ridership and for transit systems across the country, from which it
has not yet fully recovered, and which is not expected in the financial plan period (see Figure 1).

Ridership continues to be below the 2019 level but has risen consistently since the pandemic.
Accordingly, the MTA’s financial plan does not assume that ridership will fully recover for any
mode of public transportation by 2030, but it does assume that it will reach a blended rate of
ridership recovery of 74 percent by the end of 2026. Combined ridership of subways, buses and
commuter railroads in 2025 was 73 percent of the 2019 level, with ridership reaching as high as
75 percent of the pre-pandemic level in July, September and December.
Some modes of travel have recovered faster than others, especially on weekends as revealed in
a recent OSC report. Paid subway ridership in 2026 is expected to be 78 percent of the 2019
level while paid bus ridership is expected to be only 60 percent of that level. MTA commuter
railroad ridership has recovered faster than subways and buses. LIRR ridership in 2026 is
expected to reach 97 percent of 2019 ridership while Metro-North’s ridership is projected at 87
percent of pre-pandemic levels. MTA Bridge and Tunnel crossings exceeded the 2019 level in
2023 and continue to grow.

Subway Ridership
As shown in Figure 2, the subway system provided 1.8 billion trips in 2015, which was a postwar
high. Ridership declined by 4 percent by 2019 and plummeted to 640 million trips in 2020 as a
result of the COVID pandemic but has recovered to 1.3 billion rides in 2025, 76 percent of
ridership in 2019. The July Plan assumes that riders will continue to return to the system,

reaching a ridership of nearly 1.4 billion by 2028, 82 percent of the 2019 level and remain at that
level through 2030.
OSC’s subway ridership dashboard shows that ridership has returned unevenly across the City.
Since September 2024, many stations in the Bronx, Upper Manhattan and outer Brooklyn have
been lagging while stations in the downtown areas of Manhattan and Brooklyn helped to fuel the
recovery.

Bus Ridership
Buses resumed charging fares in August 2020 after a period of free bus rides at the outset of the
pandemic. Paid bus ridership then recovered quicker than other modes early in the pandemic as
essential workers utilized the system. However, combined paid bus ridership for New York City
Transit (NYCT) and MTA Bus dropped to around 60 percent of the 2019 level starting in
September 2023 as fare evasion rose as high as 51 percent in the second quarter of 2024.
Progress in reducing fare evasion resulted in paid bus ridership increasing to 68 percent of the
2019 level in July 2025 but then slipped to an average of 60 percent of 2019 through August
2026 as fare evasion started to rise again.
Combined paid ridership for NYCT and MTA Bus declined 12 percent from 2015 to 2019, from
776 million to 680 million, but then dropped to 255 million in 2020 as the bus system was
impacted by the pandemic and was free for a large portion of the year, primarily serving essential
workers. Paid bus ridership increased to 427 million in 2023 but then dropped to 409 million in
2024, attributed to increased fare evasion.

The MTA notes that paid bus ridership continues to be impacted by fare evasion. The MTA
estimates 49 percent of bus riders did not pay the fare in the second quarter of 2026, higher than
the 45 percent estimated in the second quarter of 2025. OSC estimates if fare evasion was
reduced by 10 percentage points from 48 percent to 38 percent in the first six months of 2026,
bus fare revenues would have been as much as $75 million higher or $150 million for the full
year. Further success in reducing fare evasion is pivotal to reducing future budget gaps.

Commuter Railroad Ridership
Ridership on the MTA’s commuter rails continues to improve. The LIRR’s paid ridership rose to
90 percent of 2019 ridership in 2025, benefiting in part from additional service to Grand Central
Madison that began in 2023. Metro-North recovered to 82 percent of pre-pandemic ridership in
2025.
LIRR ridership fell to 30.3 million in 2020, a 67 percent drop from 91.1 million in 2019, which had
the highest recorded ridership level since 1949. By 2025, ridership had increased to nearly 82
million. (see Figure 4). The July Plan expects ridership to continue to recover, reaching 89.2
million in 2030, which is just 2 percent lower than the 2019 level.

Figure 4 also shows ridership trends for Metro-North Railroad. Ridership reached a record of
86.6 million in 2019, before dropping 69 percent to 27.2 million in 2020, but increased to
71.4 million in 2025. The July Plan expects Metro-North ridership to reach 76 million in 2028 and
78 million in 2029, the first full year of Penn Station Access service. In 2030, ridership is
expected to be at 90 percent of the 2019 level.

MTA Bridge and Tunnel Crossings
MTA Bridge and Tunnel crossings reached a record 329 million in 2019. Crossings fell 23 percent
in 2020 to 253 million, a smaller drop than transit and commuter rail ridership. Crossings
exceeded the 2019 level in 2023 and set records in 2024 (337 million) and 2025 (339 million).
The July Plan expects crossings to drop to 334 million in 2026 due to winter storms and then
increase by 1.6 percent by 2030 to just under the 2025 level.

MTA Customer Satisfaction

satisfaction for all modes of transit in the fall and spring. Subway service satisfaction dipped to
47 percent in the spring of 2024 due to customer safety concerns. That month, the Governor
announced a subway safety plan that included an increased police presence and bag checks.
Since that time customer satisfaction has steadily risen to 63 percent in the spring of 2026 (see

Satisfaction with local, limited and select bus services reached its lowest point in the fall of 2024

Changes Since the MTA’s February Plan
In February 2026, the MTA projected a balanced budget for 2026 and budget gaps of
$160 million in 2027, $243 million in 2028 and $306 million in 2029. The February Plan included
fare and toll yields that would rise by 4 percent in March 2027 and March 2029 that would require
future MTA Board approval, unidentified savings actions starting in 2027 that increase to
$173 million annually by 2029 and $200 million annually from 2026 through 2029 in expected
Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) reimbursements of COVID-19 expenses.
On July 29, 2026, the MTA released a midyear update to its 2026 budget and a four-year
financial plan with a preliminary budget for 2027. The July Plan maintains balance in 2026 but
forecasts cumulative gaps from 2027 to 2029 to be $800 million higher than in February, largely
due to increases in unanticipated expenses. The gaps are now expected to be $295 million in
2027, $507 million in 2028, $707 million in 2029, and $897 million in 2030 (see Figure 6).

Overall, revenues are now anticipated to be greater than in February. Higher than anticipated
fare and toll revenue is expected to improve the Authority’s fiscal outlook by a net of $74 million
through 2029. The assumption that fare and toll yields would rise by 4 percent in March 2027 and
March 2029 remains unchanged in the July Plan. The MTA also received $590 million of COVID
reimbursement from FEMA earlier than anticipated, which primarily contributed to other operating

10

revenues increasing in 2026 and decreasing in 2027 and 2028, offsetting amounts previously
projected in those years.
Most significantly, dedicated taxes and subsidies increased in the July Plan primarily due to
higher estimates for Metropolitan Mass Transportation Operating Assistance (MMTOA), payroll
mobility tax (PMT), and casino license and gaming tax revenues. MMTOA receipts forecasted in
the July Plan reflect a higher appropriation in the FY 2026-2027 State Enacted Budget of
$542 million through 2029. Reflecting actual receipts through May 2026, PMT receipts dedicated
to the operating budget are also forecasted to be $109 million higher in 2026 than projected in
the February Plan but projections for subsequent years remained unchanged.
The July Plan reflects an update to the forecast of gaming tax revenues from the three downstate
casinos awarded licenses in December 2025. Since the licenses were awarded earlier than
anticipated and the first casino opened in April 2026, recurring gaming tax revenues will begin
sooner than forecasted, resulting in a net of $195 million more revenue through 2029 than
projected in the February Plan.
Despite the more positive revenue picture, expenses have increased to fully offset these gains
and add to the MTA’s out-year budget gaps. Labor expenses are expected to be $1.1 billion
higher over four years, primarily due to increased costs from a higher-than-expected wage
increase for several LIRR unions and the assumed application of that pattern to the rest of the
MTA workforce, as well as higher pension costs from State changes to Tier 6 pension benefits, all
totaling $723 million during the 2026 to 2029 period. Compared to the February Plan, overtime
expenses also increased by $72 million in 2026, reflecting higher overtime needs for January
and February winter storms, as well as increases related to toll evasion enforcement efforts and
sporting events.
Due to higher premium growth rates than assumed in the February Plan, health and welfare
expenses for both employees and retirees are expected to increase by a total of $257 million
from 2026 through 2029.
The July Plan expected the cost of energy to be higher from 2026 through 2029, even as costs
are expected to decline year-over-year in 2027, reflecting the impact of spikes in electric power
rates and fuel costs due to weather and supply pressure from the Middle East. Electric power
expenses are expected to be $239 million higher over four years, while fuel costs are expected
to be $135 million higher. Claims expenses are also expected to be an average of $151 million
higher annually during the 2026 to 2029 period due to updated actuarial projections.
Compared to the February Plan, professional services contracts expenses are expected to be a
total of $388 million higher through 2029. Expenses are rising primarily due to higher NYCT
maintenance needs for new ADA-compliant elevators; investments in bus collision avoidance
technology; bus cameras for on-board surveillance; passenger information displays and MTA HQ
expenses for investments in enterprise technology modernization; data and analytics capabilities;
and outsourced OMNY customer service and retail network operations.
Debt service expenses were $149 million lower than the February Plan due to updated timing
expectations of debt issuances for the 2020-2024 Capital Program and refunding savings.

Operating Budget Trends
The July Plan projects that the MTA’s operating budget revenues will total $22.6 billion in 2027,
and expenses are expected to be $22.8 billion, resulting in a projected budget gap of
$295 million. Revenues include a planned fare and toll increase in March 2027. Expenses
include debt service on bonds, but exclude debt backed by capital lockbox funds issued to
finance the capital program.
Revenues have shifted to a greater dependence on taxes and subsidies since the onset of the
pandemic, as the MTA was provided with substantial operating support from the State to manage
the decline in ridership. As shown in Figure 7, 38 percent of the MTA’s 2027 revenues are
expected to come from fare and toll revenue (26 percent and 12 percent, respectively). By
comparison, in 2019, just over half of the MTA’s revenues came from fares and tolls. Dedicated
taxes enacted by the State will account for 43 percent of total revenue, up from nearly 37 percent
in 2019, and State and local subsidies and other funding agreements will contribute another
8 percent. Other operating revenues, which include paratransit funding from the City, advertising
and concessions, make up 11 percent.

average of 1.5 percent annually between 2027 and 2030. For comparison purposes, the annual
growth rate from 2015 to 2019, which saw two fare hikes, was 2.2 percent.
The MTA anticipates that baseline farebox and toll revenues will increase by an average of
0.5 percent annually between 2027 and 2030, excluding projected fare and toll increases in 2027

Total farebox revenues in 2030 would still be 9 percent below 2019, prior to planned fare
increases, and 2 percent lower if those fare increases are enacted. Commuter railroad fare
revenues in 2025 were the closest to the 2019 level at 89 percent while subway fare revenue
was at 83 percent of 2019. Bus and paratransit fare revenue was just 72 percent of the prepandemic level in 2025.
Dedicated tax revenue, the largest revenue contributor to the operating budget, is expected to
increase by an average of 1.5 percent annually between 2027 and 2030. The recently enacted
State budget made changes to the distribution of funds between the MMTOA account and the
Mass Transportation Trust Fund (MTTF). Effective April 1, 2026, 85 percent of sales tax receipts
that were deposited in the MMTOA will now be deposited in the MTTF to provide more debt
service coverage for MTTF bonds. Together, these revenues are expected to increase by less
than 1 percent annually between 2027 and 2030. This forecast is aligned with the enacted State
budget projection, which is subject to change. Combined MMTOA and MTTF revenues far
exceeded this rate of growth from 2021 through 2025 (7.8 percent).
PMT revenue that benefits the operating budget is expected to increase by 4 percent in 2026
and then grow by 1.4 percent on average annually between 2027 and 2030 as wage
disbursements are expected to continue to grow, albeit slowly.

Collections from real estate transaction taxes rose by 26 percent in 2025 and are projected to
increase by 10 percent in 2026 after declining by 42 percent from 2022 to 2024. These revenues
are expected to increase by 13 percent in 2027. Real estate transaction tax revenues are
expected to increase by 3 percent annually through 2030 to approach the 2022 level. The MTA
generally stays close to New York City’s projections for the City portion of the real estate
transaction taxes in its budget. In the latest report on the City’s financial plan, OSC noted that
collections of these taxes may be $100 million lower than the City planned in every year of its
financial plan; however, the MTA is still using January figures, which suggest a more cautionary
growth rate, therefore OSC does not view these revenues as a potential risk.
The MTA began receiving a new revenue source, casino gaming taxes, in 2026 as one casino is
fully operational expected to bring in $75 million in 2026 and $140 million in 2027, 2028 and
2029. The remaining two casinos are expected to be open in 2030, bringing forecasted casino
tax revenue to $300 million that year.

Expenditure Trends
The MTA anticipates baseline expenditures are expected to increase by 3.4 percent annually
between 2026 and 2030. This growth is being fueled by nondiscretionary expense growth. One
of the largest cost drivers is for health and welfare costs for active employees and retirees, which
are expected to rise by an average annual increase of 7.4 percent. Other factors include an
annual average increase of 7 percent in paratransit contract costs and a 6.6 percent average
annual increase in the cost of materials and supplies (mostly from a 12 percent increase in 2027
driven by inflation in the cost of materials associated with fleet maintenance and modifications at
the LIRR). Debt service paid out of the operating budget is also expected to increase by
6 percent annually between 2026 and 2030.
Payroll costs are expected to increase by 2.4 percent annually during this period, reflecting
pattern settlements based on a projected net 3.8 percent increase in the first year of new labor
contracts that was included in an agreement reached in the wake of the recent Long Island Rail
Road strike. The MTA projects wage increases of 2 percent annually after the first year for all
contracts.
One area of personnel costs that has grown is overtime. MTA overtime spending paid out of the
operating budget (non-reimbursable overtime) grew by 72 percent between 2013 and 2018,
reaching a record of nearly $1.1 billion. The increase was mainly driven by high-priority
maintenance during the Subway Action Plan because of infrastructure failures and covering
positional vacancies.
In 2019, non-reimbursable overtime declined by 9 percent to $974 million as the MTA took
actions to better manage its overtime, including instituting electronic approval forms for some
workers and instituting consistent written supervisory approvals. In 2020, it fell another 7 percent
to $910 million as service was reduced during the pandemic. From 2020 through 2023, a period
beset by staffing reductions and increased sick leave, non-reimbursable overtime rose every
year, increasing by 27 percent and reaching a record level of $1.2 billion in 2023, higher than the
previous record set in 2018 (see Figure 11). The cost of non-reimbursable overtime dropped by 4
percent in 2024 but in 2025, it returned close to the record level set in 2023.

The July Plan assumes non-reimbursable overtime will decrease by 13 percent in 2026 as hiring
picks up, reducing the need for overtime, and measures are taken to reduce unneeded overtime.
Non-reimbursable overtime spending is expected to decrease by another 9 percent in 2027 but
then rise an average of 1 percent annually in 2028 through 2030, which may be unrealistic. As
shown in the Potential Budget Risks section of the report, it is possible that non-reimbursable
overtime will be more than $200 million higher than planned in 2026 if current trends continue
through the end of the year, although OSC estimates this additional spending will be offset by
payroll savings as has happened in recent years.
Capital reimbursable overtime which is paid out of the capital budget and is less sensitive to staff
vacancy levels, nearly doubled from $159 million in 2013 to $315 million in 2018 and dropped to
$196 million in 2021 as construction work dropped during the pandemic. In 2025, capital
reimbursable overtime hit a record level of $318 million but is expected to decline by 19 percent
to $256 million in 2026 and stay at that level through 2030.

Nondiscretionary Expenses
Nondiscretionary expenses, which include nonpayroll labor costs such as health and welfare;
pensions and fringe benefits; debt service, energy, and paratransit costs are expected to make
up about half of operating expenses in 2026 and are expected to increase 5 percent annually
between 2026 and 2030, reaching 52 percent of all expenses by that year. Discretionary
expenses, by comparison, are expected to increase by 2 percent during that period. The MTA is
in the midst of a multi-year savings program as described later in this report. The MTA should
continue to identify cost efficiencies in the discretionary portion of the budget over which it has
the most control and evaluate new strategies to bend the cost curve for nondiscretionary items.

Staffing Levels
The size of the MTA’s workforce has fluctuated based on the Authority’s financial position and
operational need since the Great Recession, as well as the time needed to hire and train for
vacant positions. Between 2008 and 2011, the MTA cut its workforce by 4,116 employees
(excluding Long Island Bus) to offset a sharp drop in revenues caused by the Great Recession.

19

The workforce then gradually increased by 8,277, about 84 percent of which were operations
and maintenance personnel, peaking at 72,800 in December 2018 (see Figure 16).
In 2019, the number of employees dropped as the MTA instituted a hiring freeze on
administrative and nonoperational positions and dropped further in 2020 as the hiring freeze
expanded to operational positions in response to fiscal pressures created by the pandemic. The
hiring freeze on operational positions was lifted in February 2021 as the MTA’s budget pressures
eased due to an unprecedented level of federal funding. However, operational struggles mounted
and the MTA initially had difficulty hiring as fast as employees were retiring or leaving (see OSC’s
report for details).
As of August 2026, the MTA workforce totaled 72,453 employees, 572 fewer than at the end of
2018. Operational and maintenance headcount that month totaled 63,077, which was 3,445
more than in December 2021, but 1,100 fewer than in December 2025. The July Plan authorizes
the MTA to hire 3,248 employees between August 2026 and December 2027 (including 2,423
operational and maintenance positions) to reach 75,476 employees, which would be a record.
The MTA then expects the number of staff to drop slightly in 2028, mostly in NYCT maintenance,
and be at 75,569 in 2030, all of which would be substantially higher than current staffing levels.

MTA Savings Program
Since 2023, the MTA has set targets for savings initiatives to help balance its budget and operate
more efficiently without affecting service. Since then, the MTA identified $100 million in recurring
savings starting in 2023, another $300 million in recurring savings starting in 2024 and an
additional $100 million in 2025 for a total savings goal of $500 million annually. The MTA has
identified another $75 million in recurring savings in 2026 and has set a goal of achieving
another $175 million in savings by 2029 for a total of $750 million annually.
The MTA monitors all savings initiatives that total $1 million or more in any fiscal year. In 2025,
expected savings initiatives totaling $403 million were monitored and the MTA reports that
$396 million of the monitored savings were achieved with variances of $7 million at NYCT which
were then filled with additional savings measures such as reimagining the role of traffic checkers
by using automated people-counting software and paratransit call center savings.
NYCT achieved $332 million of monitored savings in 2025, mostly in maintenance ($130 million)
and administration ($86 million). More than one-fourth of the maintenance savings ($36 million)
were achieved by extending the overhaul cycle of railcars from every six years to every six and
one-half years and another $28 million was saved by implementing a new schedule that better
matches the electronic maintenance workforce to the needed work. NYCT planned on $89 million
in administrative savings in 2025 but was able to save $86 million as it fell short in achieving the
full $5 million targeted for facility savings due to several factors including lease restrictions.
The Long Island Rail Road was able to achieve all $4 million of savings it monitored in 2025,
saving that amount in maintenance costs for its train fleet.
Metro-North achieved all $28 million of the savings it monitored in 2025 mostly from controlling
maintenance overtime ($16 million) and by allocating track Maintenance of Way work between
the operating and capital budgets ($6 million).
The MTA Bus Company achieved all $10 million of its monitored actions in 2025, and MTA
Bridges and Tunnels saved all $12 million of its monitored actions.

Potential Budget Risks
The MTA has identified various budgetary risks to its financial plan, a feature of the MTA’s
budgets that allow for greater clarity on potential sources of budget volatility that may arise over
the financial plan period. These risks include approval and implementation of proposed fare and
toll increases and timing of casino revenues. These self-identified risks are reasonable concerns
and should be monitored for their impact on its financial plan. At the same time, OSC has
identified additional potential budget risks in the review of the July Financial Plan.
The first and largest of the MTA-identified risks are macroeconomic trends that could increase
recurring costs or reduce revenues. An economic slowdown could adversely impact ridership and
tax revenues. The MTA estimates that a slowdown could lower dedicated tax revenues by
$300 million to $600 million annually.
The labor contract for the TWU, the MTA’s largest union, which usually sets the pattern for the
MTA’s other labor unions, expired in May 2026. Several unions at the LIRR recently settled a
contract after a brief strike that followed the pattern set by the TWU but added a fourth year that
will pay an effective increase of 3.8 percent, net of agreed upon labor savings. The July Plan
assumes the first year of successor contracts across the MTA will follow the LIRR pattern costing
$92 million in 2026, rising to $187 million by 2030. The July Plan continues to assume annual
wage increases of 2 percent for unsettled and future labor agreements after that first year. The
MTA estimates that each 1 percent over what has been budgeted for labor contracts will cost
$100 million.
The July Plan also identified a risk in the timing of receipt of recurring casino revenue, as the
financial plan relies on $140 million annually in 2027 through 2029 and $300 million in 2030. The
MTA currently believes that the expectation for 2027 through 2029 seems to be achievable but
delays in opening the remaining two casinos scheduled for 2030 could put $160 million at risk
that year.
OSC projects other risks and offsets total savings of $202 million in 2026, $86 million in 2027,
slightly higher costs of $10 million in 2028 and budget savings of $38 million in 2029 and
$85 million in 2030 (see Figure 17). For example, overtime costs paid out of its operating budget
through August 2026 were $821 million, $141 million higher than forecast in the July Plan for the
same period. Overtime spending would have to average $43 million monthly for the remainder of
2026 to meet the current budget of $993 million, when it averaged $103 million a month in the
first eight months of the year. Since vacancies and availability challenges are still leading to
higher-than-planned overtime at NYCT, this may be unrealistic. As a result, OSC forecasts that
overtime costs could be $225 million higher than planned in 2026 and $200 million higher in
2027 with the risk declining to $150 million in 2030.

Status of MTA Capital Programs
The MTA’s capital programs, which generally span five-year periods, are critical to bringing the
overall system into a state of good repair, maintaining normal replacement of assets, and
improving and expanding the system to meet its riders’ needs. (For a broad overview of the
MTA’s capital program, see OSC’s recent report). Capital programs are funded through various
sources, including debt issued by the Authority.

Capital Commitments
Historically, the MTA has multiple capital programs active at the same time. Generally, it takes
more than five years to commit (i.e., award) all the projects in a capital program to contractors
and additional time to complete the work. When commitments take more time, there is greater
potential for capital disinvestment to occur and costs to rise over time, leaving assets to
deteriorate and limiting what can be accomplished with available funding.
For various reasons, some of which were outside the MTA’s control, like additions made in
response to Superstorm Sandy and funding disputes with partners, both the 2010-2014 and
2015-2019 capital programs have taken longer than seven years to commit all projects. As of
September 1, 2026, both plans have commitments that exceeded 96 percent of the total amount.
The 2020-2024 capital program will almost certainly take more than seven years to commit a
similar rate of its projects as well, given past delays from uncertainty over the availability and
timing of revenue for the program.
As of September 1, 2026, $41.2 billion (74 percent) of the $55.4 billion 2020-2024 program has
been committed (see Figure 18). Six years into the 2015-2019 and 2010-2014 capital programs,
which were smaller than the 2020-2024 program, more than 80 percent of the program value had
been committed (excluding Superstorm Sandy work). In total, $16.5 billion (out of more than
$120 billion) in the MTA’s capital programs from 2010 through 2024 remains to be committed.

Debt Outstanding
The amount of MTA long-term debt outstanding more than doubled from 2000 to 2010, from
$11.4 billion in 2000 to $29 billion in 2010, as the MTA funded a significant portion of its capital
programs with bonds. The pace of growth slowed to 22 percent from 2010 to 2019, to reach
$35.4 billion, as support from other funding partners rose when compared to the decade prior.
Since 2019, debt has risen to accommodate increased capital spending, reaching $40.9 billion in
long-term debt paid from the operating budget in 2024, a 16 percent increase since 2019, albeit
a much slower pace than seen from 2000 to 2010 (see Figure 23). A substantial portion of
recently issued debt has been backed by the PMT and aid trust account receipts such as taxi
and auto fees, which are provided to the MTA outside of the State’s appropriation process, with
residual funds flowing through to the operating budget.

Debt outstanding paid out of the operating budget has started to flatten because of the MTA’s
usage of the capital lockbox to issue debt. In 2025, outstanding operating budget debt was
$800 million lower than in 2024 at $40.1 billion.
In 2019, the State elected to provide the MTA with funds from congestion pricing, which charges
a toll for all vehicles entering the Manhattan congestion relief zone south of 60th Street, a portion
of New York City and State sales taxes capturing internet marketplace sales and the real estate
transfer tax on properties sold for over $2 million.
In 2022, the MTA began issuing capital lockbox debt backed by City sales tax revenues
dedicated for the 2020-2024 and successor capital programs. These sales tax contributions are
the result of the elimination of a tax exemption for third-party internet marketplace providers from
collecting and remitting New York sales tax from transactions conducted on their sites. A total of

$3.6 billion has been issued through 2024 and the MTA has indicated that it cannot issue any
more of this type of debt given the limits of the revenue source.
In January 2025, the MTA issued $1.6 billion in lockbox bonds backed by New York City real
estate transfer tax revenues. In September 2026, the MTA issued the remaining $770 million of
its bond capacity from this credit.
After a pause in the implementation of congestion pricing, the MTA approved a lower toll in
November 2024 for implementation in January 2025. The MTA plans to phase in toll increases in
2028 and 2031, which is expected to bring in sufficient revenue to fund $15 billion for the 20202024 capital program. The MTA’s debt forecast includes $13 billion of future bonding backed by
congestion relief zone revenues. The MTA has issued $750 million in PMT Bond Anticipation
Notes (BANs), $500 million in TBTA Subordinate BANs and has borrowed $500 million as a loan,
all of which are expected to be permanently financed with future congestion relief zone revenues
and debt. The MTA may issue lockbox bonds backed by those congestion relief zone revenues in
2027, including retiring the loan and the BANs, following a period in which revenues are collected
consistently and are reaching certain projection thresholds, thereby providing confidence to
potential investors.
The recently approved 2025-2029 capital program is expected to be funded with another
$23.9 billion of proceeds from lockbox debt backed by the expanded PMT approved last year.
The MTA expects to issue this debt starting in 2027 through 2034 and use $7.6 billion of cash
payments sourced from these funds known as PAYGO expected to be used through 2035. The
MTA’s current debt issuance forecast does not include any additional debt that may be issued to
self-fund $3 billion of the 2025-2029 capital program, based on the MTA’s assumption that it will
achieve its cost savings target.
Including capital lockbox debt for the 2020-2024 and 2025-2029 capital programs, which is kept
outside of the operating budget to eliminate any impact on operational spending, the MTA
forecasts debt outstanding to rise from $45.3 billion in 2025 to $88.9 billion in 2033.
The use of lockbox capital funds will reduce the growth in non-lockbox long-term debt
outstanding. This debt is expected to decrease from $40.2 billion in 2025 to $39.5 billion in 2028
before starting to rise to $47.8 billion in 2033 as the MTA issues planned non-lockbox debt for its
2025-2029 program.
The generation of capital lockbox funds for debt is critical to managing the overall debt load of
the MTA and its impact on debt service and its operating budget. Using $7.5 billion of the 20252029 lockbox funds for PAYGO to fund projects also keeps pressure off the operating budget.
Bonding $7.5 billion would cost $500 million annually for 30 years, so this approach provides
$7 billion in long-term savings, prior to discounting for future value.
Future declines in debt outstanding for non-lockbox debt are reliant on capital lockbox debt
providing funds to pay for capital projects. Capital lockbox debt is expected to grow from
5 percent of debt outstanding in 2023 to 47 percent by 2036, which is a fundamental component
of the MTA’s plan to manage its long-term debt.

The MTA plans to issue these bonds in the 2029 to 2031 period to permanently finance shortterm BANs used to fund projects. The debt forecast includes the issuance of $12.8 billion of
bonds for the 2025-2029 capital program that are paid for out of the operating budget, including
$3 billion issued for Bridge and Tunnel (B&T) projects (which is fully funded by B&T operating
revenues) and $9.8 billion for transit and commuter projects issued over the 2030 to 2034 period.

Debt Service and Debt Burden
Debt service is the combined principal and interest payment for debt obligations. Debt service on
issued bonds is a long-term obligation, often extending 30 years or more after issuance. When
debt-servicing costs are paid from the operating budget and not properly managed, it can
potentially crowd out operating spending of other types, as there is limited control over the ability
to reduce these costs over time. The MTA’s goal is to keep its debt paid from the operating
budget at an average of 15 percent of the budget, a manageable portion of operating costs.
Debt service (including lockbox debt service for both the 2020-2024 and 2025-2029 capital
programs) is projected to reach $6.9 billion by 2034 (see Figure 24), more than double the cost
of debt service in 2025.

Non-lockbox debt service between 2025 and 2034 is also expected to rise by $1.7 billion to
$4.2 billion (72 percent) mostly due to the MTA still having to fund a portion of the 2020-2024
capital program and $12.8 billion in budgeted debt to fund the 2025-2029 capital program.
Lockbox debt service for both programs is expected to rise from $273 million in 2025 to
$2.8 billion in 2034.
Part of the anticipated rise in debt service for non-lockbox debt is also due to choices over the
structuring of repayment of the debt, a concern OSC has noted in prior reports. As noted earlier,
the MTA’s debt service forecast includes the issuance of $637 million for the 2015-2019 capital
program and another $4.7 billion in anticipated debt for a total of $5.4 billion backed by the PMT
for the 2020-2024 and 2015-2019 transit and commuter rail capital projects. The MTA expects to
31

start issuing BANs for the 2020-2024 capital contribution in November 2026, and the long-term
bonds to pay back these BANs would not start to be issued until 2029 with deferred principal for
10 years meaning, in total, principal would not be paid for 13 years. The deferral of principal is
limited to the debt paid out of the operating budget for the 2020-2024 capital program. For the
2025-2029 capital program, non-lockbox debt is projected to be issued as 30-year level debt with
no principal deferral. Although not impacting the operating budget, the MTA also assumes that
principal on bonds backed by congestion pricing revenues would be deferred for the first three
years of the 34-year life of the bonds. These bonds were previously assumed in the July 2025
plan to be issued with level debt service on 30-year bonds.
The MTA’s debt forecast includes the debt service to cover $9.8 billion in bonding to help fund
the transit and commuter portion of the 2025-2029 capital program. Debt service on this bonding
is expected to begin in 2030, rise to $538 million by 2033 and peak at $639 million starting in
2035.
The latest amendment of the 2020-2024 capital program assumes $10 billion in federal formula
funding and another $4.8 billion from competitive grants, assuming high levels of funding
authorized by the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act, which expired in September 2026.
When compared to the previous funding levels, MTA estimates that formula funding could be
$250 million lower annually or $1.25 billion over federal fiscal years 2027 through 2031 if the
proposed transit authorization is approved. Other federal funding sources for transit could also
see reductions.
The 2025-2029 capital program assumes that the federal government will provide $14 billion in
total federal funding, which is uncertain given that the extension of funding will expire in
December 2026. If a proposed long-term funding authorization were to remain at prior funding
levels, the federal funding to the 2025-2029 program could be lower by at least $750 million
covering the last three years of the program. In the absence of another funding source, finding
additional capital savings or deferring needed projects, the MTA might have to increase its
bonding by that amount to fill this funding gap which could lead to $50 million in higher debt
service costs by 2033.
Also, the MTA estimates that the cost of replacing rolling stock in the 2025-2029 capital program
could be $1 billion higher from the impact of federal tariffs on imported materials needed to build
rolling stock. If the MTA chooses, new bonding to cover these additional expenses could cost
$65 million in debt service by 2033, however it has indicated it will fund these capital projects
more efficiently, or alternatively, it could purchase fewer train cars and buses.
As noted in earlier OSC reports, the MTA has used the deferral of principal for as long as 20
years to provide short-term operating budget relief while pushing costs up for future generations.
As mentioned, the MTA is currently assuming that bonding backed by operating budget revenue
for the 2025-2029 capital program will have level debt service payments. OSC encourages the
MTA to avoid deferral financing techniques for its 2020-2024 program so that the overall financial
impact on the agency does not increase over time and supports the use of level payments for the
2025-2029 capital program.
