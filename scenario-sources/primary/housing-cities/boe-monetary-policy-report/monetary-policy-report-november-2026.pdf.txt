Monetary policy at the Bank of England
The objectives of monetary policy
The Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) sets monetary policy to keep inflation low and stable,
which supports growth and jobs. Subject to maintaining price stability, the MPC is also
required to support the Government’s economic policy.
The Government has set the MPC a target for the 12-month increase in the Consumer Prices
Index of 2%. The 2% inflation target is symmetric and applies at all times. The MPC’s remit
recognises, however, that the actual inflation rate will depart from its target as a result of
shocks and disturbances, and that attempts to keep inflation at target in these circumstances
may cause undesirable volatility in output.

The Monetary Policy Report
The MPC is committed to clear, transparent communication. The Monetary Policy Report is a
key part of that.
In November 2025 we made some changes to the structure and content of the Report so that
it reflects better the wide range of inputs that are informing monetary policy, as explained in
this Quarterly Bulletin article. The purpose of the document is to set out the analysis that
informed policy discussions.
The Report is produced quarterly by Bank staff under the guidance of the members of the
MPC. It has been prepared and published by the Bank of England in accordance with section
18 of the Bank of England Act 1998.

Monetary Policy Summary
At its meeting ending on 4 November 2026, the Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) voted by a
majority of 8–1 to increase Bank Rate by 0.25 percentage points, to 4%. One member voted
to increase Bank Rate by 0.5 percentage points, to 4.25%.
The conflict in the Middle East is now in its ninth month. Global energy prices have continued
to rise from an already elevated level. UK CPI inflation increased to 3.5% in September. It is
now expected to rise to around 5% by the middle of next year. It is then projected to fall as the
impact of rising energy prices on 12-month inflation rates fades, and still restrictive monetary
policy bears down on inflation.
While the direct effects of higher energy prices are clear, and will continue to feed into firm
and household energy bills, there remains limited evidence of knock-on effects on other prices
and wages. However, the degree of inflationary pressure in the pipeline is now sufficient to
warrant an increase in Bank Rate. Activity data in recent months have generally been stronger
than expected, adding to the case for modestly tighter monetary policy.
Monetary policy is not on a pre-set path. The Committee will continue to assess the evidence
at each meeting, in particular how likely high energy prices are to feed through to other prices
and the economy more broadly, by monitoring the evolution of underlying pay, pricing
behaviour, and slack. The Committee will adjust Bank Rate as necessary to ensure CPI
inflation returns to the 2% target in the medium term.

Monetary Policy Overview
Global energy prices have risen materially further since July from already high levels and are
continuing to have a large effect on the outlook for UK inflation.
CPI inflation was 3.5% in September and is set to rise further in coming months. That is
mostly because of the direct effects of higher global energy prices on fuel and household
utility prices.
The MPC is setting monetary policy to make sure that the effects of higher energy prices do
not become embedded in inflation, so that inflation returns to the 2% target and stays there.
At this meeting, the MPC voted to raise Bank Rate by 0.25 percentage points to 4%. The
MPC’s approach to setting interest rates, including today’s decision, is based on two key
judgements.

Key policy judgement 1
Despite limited evidence so far of knock-on effects from higher energy prices, the
degree of inflationary pressure in the pipeline is now sufficient to warrant an increase
in Bank Rate.
While the direct effects of higher energy prices are already clear in UK consumer prices, and
will continue to feed through into households’ and companies’ energy bills, there remains
limited evidence of knock-on effects on other prices or wages. Indirect effects arising from
companies at home and abroad passing on increases in their energy costs through supply
chains have been limited, including for food (Box B: The outlook for food price inflation). And
there are few signs yet of changes in wage or price-setting behaviour that would feed through
into broad-based inflationary pressures – often referred to as second-round effects (Box A: An
update on evidence for second-round effects from the rise in energy prices).
But the further rise in energy prices since July means that, all else equal, it is likely that
inflationary pressures will be stronger and more persistent than previously expected. With
companies facing bigger increases in their costs, and households facing a larger reduction in
the purchasing power of their pay, companies are more likely to respond to higher energy
prices by raising wages and prices.
Companies and households may be particularly responsive to higher energy prices at
present, which could amplify the impact on other prices and wages. Energy prices are highly
visible, and headline inflation is already around levels at which responsiveness has increased

Bank of England

Page 6

in the past. Companies’ and households’ experience of high inflation over the past five years
may also have made them more likely to raise prices and bargain for higher wages in
response to the current energy price shock.
In July, the MPC judged that weakness in economic activity and the labour market would help
contain the knock-on impacts of higher energy prices by limiting companies’ pricing power
and workers’ ability to bargain for higher wages. Since then, economic activity has been more
resilient than expected and there have been signs of stabilisation in the labour market (Box C:
Recent resilience in UK economic activity). That has increased the risk that higher energy
prices will feed through into broad-based inflationary pressures.
Setting aside the effects of higher energy prices, the latest data suggest that domestic
inflationary pressures may have stopped easing. Several measures of services price inflation
and wage growth, which had been falling since before the start of the conflict in the Middle
East, have flattened off in recent months.
Taking all of the evidence together, the MPC judges that the degree of inflationary pressure in
the pipeline is now sufficient to warrant an increase in Bank Rate.

Key policy judgement 2
Where Bank Rate goes from here will depend on how the likely strength of inflationary
pressures evolves.
As higher energy prices continue to work their way through the economy, inflation is expected
to rise to around 5% by the middle of 2027 before starting to fall back towards the MPC’s 2%
target.
How quickly inflation will fall is uncertain and depends on a range of factors that will affect the
strength of inflationary pressures (Section 3.1). These include the scale and duration of the
rise in energy prices, how wage and price-setting behaviour respond, and how resilient
demand is in the face of higher energy prices and tighter financial conditions – as well as on
the response of monetary policy.
To help illustrate some of the uncertainties around the likely strength of inflationary pressures,
the Report contains two scenarios alongside a central projection that is judged to provide a
reasonable baseline for the economic outlook (Section 3.2).
The adverse inflation scenario assumes that energy prices rise sharply again and remain
elevated for a prolonged period. This higher path for energy prices is assumed to lead to
much stronger second-round effects than in the central projection.
The weaker demand scenario assumes that the recent resilience in activity does not
continue in the face of high energy prices, for example because there are limits to how

Bank of England

Page 7

much households can maintain spending by cutting saving, or because businesses
become less able to absorb higher costs as their financial resources are depleted. In this
scenario, demand is assumed to be weak enough to prevent any second-round effects
from emerging.
Monetary policy will need to adjust depending on the likely strength of inflationary pressures.
For example, if substantial second-round effects appear likely to emerge, monetary policy
would have to be tighter than otherwise. But if evidence continues to indicate that secondround effects will be limited, then monetary policy would be looser.
The model-based policy simulations in Section 3.3 of the Report help illustrate how monetary
policy may need to respond depending on the degree of inflationary pressure. These rules
inform the MPC’s assessment, but do not translate mechanically into the Committee’s
decisions.
For the central projection, the illustrative policy rules overall are broadly similar to the
market curve in the near team but somewhat lower after that.
For the adverse inflation scenario, inflationary pressures are stronger than in the central
projection and the illustrative policy rules are materially tighter than those for the central
projection.
And for the weaker demand scenario, inflationary pressures are weaker than in the central
projection, mainly in the second half of the forecast period. As a result, the illustrative policy
rules are similar to those for the central projection in the first part of the forecast but looser
further out.
The Committee will continue to assess the evidence on how the likely strength of inflationary
pressures is evolving at each meeting and will adjust Bank Rate as necessary to ensure CPI
inflation returns to the 2% target in the medium term.

1: Current economic conditions
1.1: Inflation
CPI inflation rose to 3.5% in September, with the direct effects of higher energy prices
accounting for much of the increase since June.
CPI inflation has risen from 2.6% in June to 3.5% in September (Chart 1.1), partly owing to
higher household electricity and gas prices (Chart 1.2). Motor fuel prices have also risen over
that period following the re-escalation of the conflict in the Middle East. Diesel and petrol
pump prices are around 40% and 30% higher, respectively, than prior to the start of the
conflict.
CPI inflation has been somewhat higher than projected in the July Report. Inflation in
September was 0.4 percentage points higher than anticipated (Chart 1.1), mainly reflecting
motor fuel prices rising rather than easing as had been projected.
Household utility bills and motor fuel prices accounted for around 60% of the overshoot of CPI
inflation relative to the 2% target in September, with services prices accounting for most of the
remainder.

Evidence of indirect energy effects has so far been limited.
Indirect effects arise as higher energy costs are passed through supply chains to consumer
prices (Box A of the July 2026 Monetary Policy Report). These effects have been smaller
than anticipated in the July Report (Chart 1.3, orange bars in left panel) and recent data

the prices of electricity and gas paid by firms have not yet risen significantly. Contacts of the
Bank’s Agents suggest that this may be partly explained by greater use of fixed-price
contracts and hedging arrangements than ahead of the energy shock following the Russian
invasion of Ukraine. Where firms have faced cost increases, evidence from the latest DMP
survey suggests that they have absorbed some of the rise by accepting lower margins (Box
A).

Services consumer price inflation rose to 3.7% in September, partly reflecting recent volatility
in airfares inflation. Having fallen gradually over recent years, measures of underlying
services price inflation have been flatter over recent months at around 4% (Chart 1.4). A
granular measure of underlying inflationary pressures, which puts more weight on prices that
take a relatively central position in the UK’s production network and are normally quite rigid,
has also been stable over that period (Munday et al (2026)).
Core goods inflation has picked up a little over recent months. Demand for AI-related products
such as memory chips has placed upward pressure on export prices, particularly in China and
advanced Asian economies (Box D of the July 2026 Monetary Policy Report), which is likely
to have contributed to some of this rise. All else equal, persistently higher UK import price
inflation means that the prices of domestically produced goods and services must rise more
slowly for overall inflation to remain at the 2% target (Box A of the February 2026 Monetary
Policy Report).
Although direct energy effects have driven much of the recent rise in headline inflation,
services price inflation continues to account for a material share of the inflation overshoot.
There is so far only limited evidence that indirect effects from higher energy are feeding
through to services price inflation. Overall, recent price data suggest that domestic inflationary
pressures remain somewhat above levels consistent with the 2% inflation target.

Wholesale energy prices have risen significantly since the July Report.
Global crude oil and natural gas markets have tightened following the re-escalation of conflict
in the Middle East and because of fears of further escalation over the coming months. While
flows through the Strait of Hormuz have increased since the summer, attacks on shipping
have raised shipping costs, including through increases in insurance premia. Attacks on
energy infrastructure in the region have also disrupted supply through alternative export
routes at times. Industry intelligence suggests that lower global demand and a pre-planned
increase in global liquefied natural gas (LNG) capacity have only partly mitigated these
disruptions. Meanwhile, European gas storage is below its 2018–25 average for this time of
year and the US Strategic Petroleum Reserve has fallen to its lowest level in several decades.
With lower inventories providing a limited buffer against further disruption, energy prices may
be more sensitive to additional supply shocks, or stronger-than-expected natural gas demand
in the event of a colder than average winter.
Wholesale energy futures prices have risen significantly since the July Report, reflecting both
tighter current supply conditions and upside risks from potential further disruption. In the 15
UK working days to 27 October, the front-month Brent crude oil futures price averaged $108

Crack spreads – the difference between refined petroleum product prices and crude oil prices

sustained, that could lead to lower refining activity and place further upward pressure on

Higher energy prices are projected to drive a further increase in CPI inflation.
Household energy prices are expected to rise further in the near term, following recent rises in
wholesale prices. The Ofgem price cap rose to £1,723 in 2026 Q4 from £1,663 in Q3 and
£1,477 in Q2. It is expected to increase to around £2,150 in 2027 Q1. Both the 2026 Q4 and
2027 Q1 figures include the temporary removal of VAT on household electricity bills from
October. These rises in the Ofgem price cap are expected to increase the contribution of
household energy prices to CPI inflation over the coming quarters, while the contribution from
motor fuel prices is expected to remain elevated over that period (Chart 1.2).
Taken together, direct energy effects are projected to contribute around 1.4 percentage points
to CPI inflation in 2027 Q1 (Chart 1.2), significantly more than anticipated in the July Report,
after accounting for the effects of measures announced in Budget 2026. In 2027 Q2, the
direct impact of higher energy prices is projected to be 1.1 percentage points larger than
expected in the July Report, after accounting for Budget 2026 measures, which are expected
to reduce CPI inflation directly by around ¼ percentage point in that quarter (Chart 1.3, left
panel and Box F).

CPI inflation is projected to rise to around 5% in 2027 Q2.
As fixed-price contracts and hedging arrangements expire, the impact of higher energy prices
on firms’ costs is expected to become more widespread. Indirect effects are expected to
contribute around ½ percentage point to CPI inflation, on average, in 2027 Q1. Although that
is less than expected at the time of the July Report (Chart 1.3, left panel), increases in
wholesale energy prices since then will raise firms’ costs by more than previously anticipated
over the coming quarters. As a result, indirect effects are expected to be larger than projected
in the July Report by 2027 Q2 (Chart 1.3, left panel).
As the indirect effects of higher energy prices build, inflation in a range of non-energy CPI
components is expected to rise. Food price inflation could pick up to around 4¾% by mid2027, largely reflecting pass-through of higher energy costs along food production and
distribution chains. The current El Niño event, and to a lesser extent this summer’s European
droughts and heatwaves, are estimated to push up food prices later in the forecast period
(Box B).
Core goods inflation could increase to over 2½% in 2027 Q2, partly reflecting building indirect
effects. Contacts of the Bank’s Agents report that higher prices for energy-intensive inputs,
alongside rising transportation costs for some firms, will push up core goods prices.
Stronger import price inflation is also expected to push up core goods inflation, given the
relatively high import intensity of these goods. Higher energy prices are expected to raise
costs for firms abroad, while strong demand for AI-related products from China and advanced
Asian economies is expected to put further upward pressure on import prices. Together, these
effects are expected to keep UK import price inflation elevated over the coming quarters.
CPI inflation is projected to average 4.9% in 2027 Q2 (Chart 1.1) as the contribution from
direct energy effects remains elevated while indirect effects build further. The near-term
outlook remains highly uncertain, and sensitive to developments in the Middle East and their
resulting impact on wholesale energy prices (Section 3).

Domestic wage and inflationary pressures
Domestic inflationary pressures are expected to remain somewhat elevated in the
near term.
Although higher energy prices are expected to drive the near-term rise in headline inflation,
underlying domestic inflationary pressures are expected to remain somewhat elevated. This
continues the recent trend in these indicators.
The latest Annual Survey of Hours and Earnings indicates that median private sector gross
weekly earnings grew by 4.1% in the year to April 2026, higher than private sector regular
average weekly earnings (AWE) growth of 3% but broadly in line with several other measures

Bank of England

Page 16

of pay growth at that time (Chart 1.7).
Timelier measures of pay growth have changed little over recent months. Private sector
regular AWE growth was 3% in August and is projected to remain around that level over the
remainder of 2026 (Chart 1.7). Meanwhile, other indicators continue to point to somewhat
stronger private sector wage growth. The DMP survey and Indeed Wage Tracker continue to
suggest pay growth of around 4%. A private sector pay proxy derived from HMRC RTI data is
also stronger than the official measure. However, part of the gap between headline AWE
growth and other measures continues to reflect compositional shifts towards lower-paid
industries. Overall, Bank staff judge that underlying wage growth remains a little above its
target-consistent rate, although there is uncertainty around that estimate (Box A of the
February 2026 Monetary Policy Report).
Higher energy and food prices are expected to increase non-labour costs for many firms in
the services sector, including airlines, hospitality and leisure services. Taken together with
underlying wage growth remaining somewhat elevated, these cost pressures are expected to
keep services price inflation around its current rate over the coming months. As indirect
effects build, services inflation will become a less clear indicator of underlying domestic
inflationary pressures. Nevertheless, given the expected pickup in core goods inflation,
domestically generated inflation is likely to remain above the level consistent with the 2%
inflation target in the near term.

Short-term inflation expectations remain elevated.
The energy shock could generate more persistent inflationary pressures if higher prices lead
households and firms to revise up their inflation expectations, which then feed through to
wage and price-setting decisions (Box A).
Short and medium-term household inflation expectations remain above their pre-conflict
levels, although they have fallen from their peaks earlier in the year (Chart 1.8, left panel).
Households’ attentiveness to inflation-related news continues to be high (Box A). Businesses’
short-term inflation expectations also remain slightly above their pre-conflict levels, while
medium-term expectations have been broadly stable (Chart 1.8, middle panel).

Report, likely reflecting higher near-term inflation expectations following the rise in energy
prices. Medium-term measures, such as the five-year, five-year forward inflation swap rate,

2: Topical policy issues
The boxes in this section highlight some of the key pieces of analysis that informed the
MPC’s discussions.

Box A: An update on evidence for second-round effects from
the rise in energy prices
Global energy prices have risen sharply since the onset of the Middle East conflict
and are now materially higher than at the time of the July Report. It will take time for
this rise in energy prices to affect domestic wages and prices. This box uses the set
of indicators laid out in Box A of the July 2026 Monetary Policy Report to examine
how any second-round effects from higher energy prices are evolving. The MPC will
continue to monitor closely the evidence as it relates to underlying pay, pricing
behaviour and slack. There continues to be little evidence of second-round effects
in the available data, but that does not rule out the possibility of stronger secondround effects in the future. The risk of larger second-round effects emerging has
increased, particularly due to the recent rise in energy prices, but also partly due to
the stabilisation in labour market slack.
When might second-round effects from higher energy prices become visible in
price and wage data?
An energy shock raises CPI inflation directly through higher motor fuel and household
energy bills, and indirectly as higher costs pass through supply chains. Second-round
effects tend to emerge later, as inflation feeds into wage and price-setting, adding to
persistent inflationary pressure (Lombardelli (2026)).
Second-round effects can materialise through different channels. Changes in firms’
and households’ price and wage expectations, changes in firms’ price and wagesetting behaviour, and the interaction between them, shape the persistence of
inflationary pressure following a rise in energy prices. Second-round effects also
depend on the initial conditions of the economy when the energy price shock occurs
(Box A of the July 2026 Monetary Policy Report).
There is a range of indicators which can assess the size of second-round effects,
though some will provide a stronger signal than others. For instance, pay settlements
data are expected to provide good evidence of the extent of any second-round effects

Bank of England

Page 32

from wage-setting, especially alongside reports from firms on whether inflation has
been a key driver of settlements. But the signal from these indicators will only become
clear once firms make changes to their wage-setting behaviour.
Policymakers need to make a judgement on the outlook for second-round effects,
ahead of their effects emerging clearly, since monetary policy works with a lag. But the
timelier data that inform this judgement can be noisy. If timelier indicators are not
currently signalling large second-round effects, policymakers need to decide whether
this means that these effects will ultimately be small, or that they are delayed and
stronger effects are still a possibility.
The assessment of prospective second-round effects is complicated by the path of
energy prices in recent months (Section 1.1). After falling back somewhat over the
course of May and June, prices have risen substantially again in recent months.
Therefore, it will take time for that most recent increase to show up in consumer prices,
even as the initial increase continues to work its way through the economy. Since
second-round effects are likely to depend in part on the size and persistence of the
energy shock, their ultimate effect is likely to materialise later than initially thought.
How will initial and current economic conditions influence the strength of
second-round effects?
The initial conditions in the economy when an energy shock hits are likely to affect the
size of second-round effects. This includes the prevailing rate of inflation, the
attentiveness of households and firms to inflation, and the extent of slack in the
economy (Table A.1). For example, weak demand, consistent with a margin of slack,
would make it harder for workers to bargain for higher wages and limit firms’ pricing
power. This would dampen the increase in firms’ costs and limit the ability of firms to
pass those costs on, thus limiting the extent of second-round effects.
The MPC judges that there was a degree of slack in the economy when the conflict in
the Middle East started earlier in the year, and that spare capacity had been increasing
prior to that. However, over recent months, the margin of slack is no longer judged to
be widening, reflecting the recent resilience in demand and employment indicators
(Box C and Section 1.2). If this resilience is sustained, to a greater degree than
anticipated in the central projection (Section 3), that could mean that there is less
overall slack. This could impact wage and price-setting in the future, increasing the risk
of larger second-round effects.
A further way to assess the implications of the energy shock is to use the self-exciting
threshold Bayesian vector autoregression (SET-BVAR) (Gaffney et al (2026)),
presented in Box C of the April 2026 Monetary Policy Report. This model allows the
effects of oil price rises to vary depending on the state of the economy when the shock

Bank of England

Page 33

hits. Bank staff have extended this model to capture oil price shocks associated
specifically with geopolitical events, using an AI-based measure of newspaper
coverage developed by Iacoviello and Tong (2026) and Verduzco-Bustos and
Zanetti (2026). The model allows staff to compare developments since the US-Iran
conflict with what typically happened after similar shocks in the past, depending on the
amount of slack in the labour market and the prevailing rate of inflation when the shock
occurred.
The results suggest that slack in the labour market has so far helped contain
inflationary pressures, with inflation and wage growth behaving more like the model’s
relatively benign historical episodes. But inflation was already elevated when the shock
hit. CPI inflation was 3% in February, close to the model’s estimated 3.1%–3.5% range
above which geopolitical oil price shocks have historically been associated with more
persistent inflation. Inflation expectations have also risen by more than would normally
be expected when inflation is low and labour market slack is present. This may in part
reflect households paying more attention to price rises when inflation is already
elevated.
When prevailing inflation is elevated, households and firms tend to be more attentive
to inflation. This means that their inflation expectations are more likely to rise, which
can in turn affect wage and price-setting decisions (Greene (2026) and Mann (2023)).
Measures of attentiveness, such as the frequency of Google searches for inflation
related terms, remain elevated and well above historical norms. References to
inflation-related terms in earnings call transcripts, which can capture firm attentiveness
to inflation, have also risen slightly but remain close to historical norms.
What is the current evidence for the strength of second-round effects from
price-setting?
Price-setting indicators are not currently signalling large second-round effects. Firms’
own-price expectations for the year ahead from the DMP survey rose to 4.0% in
October, up 0.3 percentage points from July, although the less volatile three-month
average was lower at 3.8% over the same period. The rise in own-price expectations
has been concentrated among more energy-intensive firms, which could mean it
reflects firms expecting to pass through the indirect effects of higher energy costs
rather than signalling the emergence of second-round effects (Chart A.1). On a threemonth average basis, firms’ own-price expectations have increased by 0.4 percentage
points since the conflict began, similar to Bank staff’s forecast of the indirect effect of
energy on CPI inflation in the year ahead. This suggests that firms are not currently
anticipating raising prices more than implied by these indirect effects, which would be
consistent with limited second-round effects.

Box B: The outlook for food price inflation
Food consumer price inflation has slowed over 2026, as domestic cost and
agricultural price pressures have eased, but could rise to around 4¾% by mid-2027
as firms pass some of their higher energy costs through to consumers. The impact
of this summer’s extreme weather in Europe is expected to be modest. However,
further ahead, the current El Niño event is expected to place persistent upward
pressure on food price inflation. These weather shocks could push up UK food price
inflation by around 2 percentage points later in the MPC’s forecast period, although
this estimate is highly uncertain. The impact on headline CPI inflation would be
more modest, reflecting the weight of food in the inflation basket. Households’
attentiveness to food prices could increase the risk of more material second-round
effects through higher inflation expectations.
Why has food price inflation eased over 2026 and what is the outlook?
Annual food and non-alcoholic beverage consumer price inflation (hereafter food price
inflation) was 1.3% in September, well below its average of 3½% over 2024–25 and
the double-digit rates recorded during 2022–23 (Chart B.1). The slowing had not been
anticipated in the April Report, when contacts of the Bank’s Agents had expected
higher energy prices to feed through to food price inflation quickly. Food price inflation
is expected to rise to a little under 2% by end-2026, significantly below the projection in
the July Report.

Easing domestic cost pressures have contributed to the slowdown in food price
inflation this year. Wage growth in the food manufacturing and retail sectors was
significantly higher than the private sector average over 2024–25, partly reflecting the
relatively high share of workers paid at or close to the NLW, which increased materially
over that period (Box E of the August 2025 Monetary Policy Report). A smaller
increase in the NLW in April 2026, alongside a broader moderation in underlying wage
growth, has reduced labour cost pressures in these sectors over the past year. The
effects of changes to employer NICs from April 2025, including the lower threshold that
disproportionately affected supermarkets given the relatively high share of their staff
employed part-time, are also judged to have faded. Increases in food packaging costs
associated with the introduction of the Extended Producer Responsibility framework
also appear to have moderated this year.
Easing pressure from some agricultural commodity prices has also weighed on food
price inflation. Global agricultural commodity prices materially affect UK food prices
because many food items are internationally traded and the UK imports large
quantities of products that cannot be produced domestically. Prices of several
agricultural commodities, including beef and cocoa, rose sharply over 2024–25

Bank of England

Page 43

because of poor weather and stronger global demand (Box E of the August 2025
Monetary Policy Report). Those pressures have since reversed, weighing on
producer prices and subsequently UK food price inflation.
Higher wholesale energy prices have so far fed through to food prices by less than
expected in the April Report. Contacts of the Bank’s Agents report greater use of fixing
and hedging arrangements for energy and some commodities than before Russia’s
invasion of Ukraine, which may have delayed the transmission of higher energy prices
to firms’ costs.
Other factors may also have limited the impact on food prices. Contacts of the Bank’s
Agents report that highly price-sensitive demand and intense competition have
constrained supermarkets’ ability to raise prices, leading many to make procurement
savings and efficiency improvements or to accept lower margins. In addition, earlier
steers from business surveys and Agents’ contacts may partly have reflected firms’
experience following Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, when large increases in energy
costs passed through rapidly to food prices. Although there has been some disruption
from the current energy shock, including to shipping and jet fuel supplies, it has been
less severe than many firms initially feared. Companies in the food supply chain have
consequently revised down their expectations for food price inflation at the end of 2026
(ASBC – October 2026).
It remains uncertain how much of the smaller than expected impact of indirect energy
effects from the initial rise in energy prices is consistent with delayed pass-through
rather than a smaller eventual impact. Wholesale energy prices have risen further
since July, however, so as firms’ hedges expire and new purchasing cycles for fertiliser
and other agricultural inputs begin, input costs are expected to rise more materially
(Section 1.1). As a result, the eventual indirect effects of the energy shock are
expected to be larger than in the July Report (Chart 1.3, orange bars in left panel). As
higher costs are passed into consumer prices, Bank staff expect food price inflation to
rise to a little under 3% in 2027 Q1 (Chart B.1) and then to around 4¾% by mid-2027.
However, contacts of the Bank’s Agents in the supermarket sector expect that
competitive pressures may limit the rise in food price inflation in 2027 (ASBC –
October 2026), which could present downside risks to Bank staff’s projections.
What are the risks to food price inflation from climate-related events?
Extreme weather events around the world can have significant effects on global
agricultural commodities and, ultimately, UK food price inflation (Copeland et al
(2026)). This year has seen droughts and heatwaves in Europe, while the current El
Niño event is expected to be stronger than most previous episodes.

Bank of England

Page 44

This summer’s European droughts and heatwaves are expected to have only a modest
impact on UK food price inflation, despite around 60% of UK food imports coming from
the region. The timing of the extreme weather limited the damage to winter cereals
such as wheat and barley, although yields of some summer crops have been more
significantly affected (Joint Research Centre (2026)). Pressures on meat and dairy
prices could also emerge with a lag if lower crop yields and grass growth increase
animal feed costs. Evidence from the similarly timed European heatwaves in 2022
points to a modest impact on food price inflation in northern European countries,
including the UK, although as these events intensify due to climate change, their
inflationary effects may increase (Kotz et al (2023)).
Further ahead, the current El Niño event is expected to place upward pressure on UK
food price inflation. El Niño is a periodic warming of sea surface temperatures in the
tropical Pacific that can affect weather patterns across major agricultural-producing
regions. The resulting changes in weather patterns could reduce crop yields in some
regions, while increasing them elsewhere. Overall, however, the current El Niño is
expected to place upward pressure on global agricultural commodity prices and, in
turn, UK food prices. Around a quarter of UK food imports, equivalent to around 10% of
total food supply, comes from El Niño-exposed regions (Redmond-King (2026)). El
Niño could also add to pressures in global energy markets by reducing biofuel
production, constraining hydroelectric and nuclear power generation, and increasing
demand for cooling and energy-intensive irrigation. Lower water levels in inland
waterways could also disrupt transport networks.
The effects on UK food price inflation are likely to emerge gradually. Disruption
associated with El Niño can take time to feed through global commodity markets and
into consumer prices (Adolfsen and Lappe (2023)). Intelligence from contacts of the
Bank’s Agents suggests that the current event could begin affecting UK food price
inflation from early 2027, initially through prices of fresh products, such as fruit and
vegetables, with broader effects then building over several quarters.
Bank staff have extended the model in Copeland et al (2026) to estimate the
combined effects of this summer’s European droughts and heatwaves and the current
El Niño event on UK inflation. This model suggests that their combined impact could
increase UK food price inflation notably, potentially by around 2 percentage points at
its peak in 2028 H2 (Chart B.2, aqua line). This estimate is based on historical
relationships between global weather events, agricultural commodity prices and food
consumer price inflation. By then, the contribution of indirect effects from the current
energy shock is expected to have largely faded. The estimated impact on headline CPI
inflation from this model is more modest, at 0.3 percentage points at the same horizon,
reflecting the weight of food in the inflation basket (orange line).

Bank of England

Page 45

These model-based estimates should not be interpreted as additional to the central
projection, as some of the anticipated effects may already be reflected in the global
agricultural commodity futures prices on which it is conditioned. The peak estimated
impacts are broadly consistent with those in the stylised scenario on El Niño-linked
droughts from the Network for Greening the Financial System.
There is considerable uncertainty around these estimates, mainly reflecting the
evolution of El Niño and its effects on global commodity markets. Bank staff judge that
risks around these model-based estimates are tilted to the upside, as historical
relationships may not be applicable to this unusually strong episode. Although the
estimated impact on headline inflation is relatively modest, higher food price inflation
could nevertheless raise household inflation expectations and lead to associated
second-round effects, given households’ attentiveness to food prices (Box A and
Anesti et al (2025)).
The current El Niño event could raise UK CPI inflation by more than is captured in the
model-based estimates in Chart B.2 through its effects on global energy markets. The
coincidence of weather and energy shocks over recent years also makes it difficult to
disentangle the effects of weather events on energy prices and inflation. The estimates
in Chart B.2 only capture the indirect effects of weather-related increases in energy
costs on food prices. Higher energy costs could also raise inflation directly and
indirectly through other CPI components.
More broadly, physical climate impacts are expected to become more frequent and
severe over time. A sequence of individually transitory shocks could interact and
compound, and greater coincidence of shocks may magnify disruptions to supply,
leading to more persistent effects on inflation.

Box C: Recent resilience in UK economic activity
The global energy price shock stemming from the continued conflict in the Middle
East has already resulted in higher UK inflation, increased uncertainty, and tighter
financial conditions. Despite these headwinds, UK GDP growth has so far been
resilient in 2026 and much stronger than anticipated at the start of the conflict. This
reflects a combination of factors including stronger global growth, support from
domestic AI-related activity, and potentially a greater than expected waning of the
impact of monetary policy tightening in previous years. The combination of these
factors has so far offset the drag from higher energy prices. However, this support is
expected to wane going forward and growth is expected to weaken in coming
quarters, as higher inflation, tighter financial conditions, and the ongoing fiscal
consolidation increasingly weigh on domestic economic activity.
Activity growth has been resilient in the year to date.
UK economic growth has been resilient in 2026 and notably stronger than expected at
the time of the April Report following the onset of the conflict in the Middle East. Bank
staff now expect four-quarter GDP growth in 2026 Q4 to be around 2.2%, up from a
little below 1.0% a year earlier and over 1 percentage point stronger than the
scenarios presented in the April Report.
The pickup in GDP growth this year is partly corroborated by private sector
surveys and does not appear to reflect one-off or volatile factors.
Unlike over 2022–25, when GDP growth was supported to a larger extent by rising
public sector output, resilience this year has been primarily driven by the private
sector, and in particular private sector services (Chart C.1). This is corroborated by
surveys of private sector activity, which have also picked up, although they remain
somewhat weaker than headline GDP growth (Section 1.2). In recent years, GDP
growth has also tended to be stronger in the first half of the year, before slowing in the
second half. However, this year, strength has persisted into the second half of the year,
suggesting any one-off or volatile factors affecting the pattern of activity recently have
not been as evident this year.
Higher energy prices will weigh somewhat on activity, but their impact is not
likely to have been large so far.
Lessons from previous energy shocks suggest that the effect of higher energy prices
on activity is relatively small (Forecast Evaluation Report (2026)). For instance, the
experience of high energy prices after 2022 demonstrated households and firms’

Bank of England

Page 48

greater ability to substitute away from energy consumption over recent years. This
meant the impact on activity of high energy prices then was smaller than first
expected, which has been incorporated into Bank staff’s improved modelling of the
impact of energy on economic activity (Albuquerque et al (2025)). In light of this,
higher energy prices from the conflict in the Middle East are expected to weigh on
activity, but their impact would only have been expected to be relatively modest so far
this year. As energy prices have risen significantly further since the July Report, Bank
staff expect the drag from energy on the level of GDP to increase to around ¼ of a per

weakens more than expected due to higher energy prices (Section 3).

Box D: Developments in UK productivity growth
Productivity growth matters for monetary policy because it affects the speed at
which the economy can grow without generating excess inflationary pressures. The
ONS recently announced a new approach for measuring productivity, which
indicates that productivity has grown more, and hours worked have grown less,
between 1997 and 2024 than previously estimated. While these revisions alter the
composition of the economy’s supply capacity over the past, they do not affect its
overall size and have limited implications for past estimates of the output gap. New
revised data beyond 2024 are not yet available, but proxy measures suggest that
productivity growth has been relatively strong over the past two years. Much of this
recent strength is likely to reflect cyclical factors which are unlikely to be sustained.
But there is also some evidence that AI adoption has contributed to the recent
pickup. Overall, significant uncertainties remain around the productivity outlook, with
material upside risks if the benefits from AI adoption increase.
The ONS has announced significant upward revisions to UK productivity
growth since the 2008 financial crisis.
In September the ONS announced a new method for measuring labour inputs in its
productivity statistics. The current ‘direct approach’ takes estimates of whole-economy
jobs and hours worked from the Labour Force Survey (LFS). But the LFS suffers from
low response rates, which can introduce volatility as well as biases. In particular, the
LFS appears to have overstated hours worked. For example, people on holiday are
less likely to respond to the survey. Imputing their hours based on previous responses
may overestimate actual hours worked. As response rates fell, the upward bias in
hours worked may have increased. Previous LFS data implausibly suggest that leave
taken has fallen below statutory minimums over the past 20 years, despite an increase
in leave allowances (Taylor and Martin (2026)).
The new ‘component approach’ estimates labour inputs using a broader range of
surveys and administrative data, drawing on different sources for jobs, workers, and
hours where they provide the strongest evidence. Hours worked are adjusted for
estimated annual leave and other absences, using alternative data sources where
these provide more reliable evidence than direct survey measures.
Annual productivity growth estimates based on the new component approach up to
2024 have been published. The ONS will publish quarterly estimates of labour
productivity under the component approach up to 2026 Q3 on 17 November. The MPC

Bank of England

Page 54

welcomes these improvements to economic measurement, which bring UK productivity
statistics in line with international best practice.
The revisions up to 2024 suggest that productivity growth has been materially stronger
since the 2008 financial crisis than previously estimated (Chart D.1). Output per job
and output per worker are little changed under the new approach, but output per hour
worked is now estimated to have increased by an average annual rate of close to 1%
over 2009–24, compared to around 0.5% prior to the measurement changes. This
suggests UK productivity growth since the financial crisis has been somewhat stronger
than in many European peer countries, though still weaker than in the US.

Most of the recent strength in productivity growth appears to have been driven by

growth due to early effects of AI adoption. The factors underpinning the cyclical
strength in productivity growth are unlikely to be sustained. In the central projection,

productivity growth by about 0.9 percentage points per year on average over the next
three years, split roughly equally between slower employment growth and faster output
growth (Yotzov et al (2026)).

Box E: The speed of monetary policy transmission to
inflation
Monetary policy is traditionally thought to work with long and variable lags. But the
speed of transmission of policy to inflation is likely to vary over time and may
depend for example on the level of inflation, the attentiveness of households and
firms to inflation and interest rates, and the scale of policy responses. This box
reviews evidence on the length of monetary policy lags and considers the
implications of potentially faster transmission for current policy strategy.
Transmission appears to have been relatively fast in the tightening cycle of 2021–
23. While transmission may be slower currently than during the previous cycle, it
could still be slightly faster than on average in the past due to elevated
attentiveness after the recent experience of high inflation. Uncertainty over the
length of monetary policy lags is high, however, and there is a range of views
among the MPC.
How long are the lags of monetary policy?
The strength and speed with which changes in Bank Rate affect the economy matter
for the economic outlook and for formulating monetary policy. Bank staff have
previously set out their views on the monetary policy transmission mechanism in Burr
and Willems (2024), and the MPC monitors the transmission of its policies on an
ongoing basis (eg Box E in the February 2026 Monetary Policy Report, Box A in the
August 2025 Monetary Policy Report, and Box C of the August 2024 Monetary
Policy Report). This box outlines updated analysis on the speed of monetary policy
transmission to inflation and discusses potential implications for current policy strategy.
Estimating the effects of monetary policy on the economy is challenging. Observed
policy rate changes and subsequent inflation movements cannot be directly linked, as
monetary policy often tightens in response to rising inflation. But higher interest rates
do not cause higher inflation. To estimate the effect of monetary policy on inflation,
researchers try to identify exogenous policy shocks that are not systematic responses
to economic developments.
The academic literature that uses monetary policy shocks yields a wide range of
estimates for the speed of transmission (Chart E.1). Some studies find peak inflation
effects after about nine months, while others suggest that it takes up to four years.
Estimates based on UK data are not systematically faster or slower than US or euroarea estimates.

The transmission mechanism may have changed over time, so newer studies could be
a better guide to current policy lags than older studies. But estimated UK monetary
policy shocks have also become much smaller over time. This may reflect policy being
set more systematically since inflation targeting began in 1992. And shocks were
particularly small in 2010–19, when Bank Rate was near the lower bound and only
changed three times in nine years. Smaller shocks are more likely to be contaminated
by other effects or noise, increasing estimation uncertainty. On balance, policymakers
may therefore want to continue to place some weight on studies based on older
samples with larger shocks, which tend to find longer lags.
More generally, policy shock studies may understate the transmission lags most
relevant for setting policy. The policy shocks identified in most studies shown in Chart
E.1 tend to be short-lived, often fading or reversing within a few quarters. This
contrasts with typically more persistent policy responses to demand or supply shocks.
Short-lived shocks may be less likely to transmit via some slower-paced channels. For
example, small and short-lived policy shocks may not fully pass through to the twoyear and five-year fixed mortgage rates set by banks. This means that the true lags of
systematic and persistent policy changes may be longer than the lags found in
monetary policy shock studies (Manuel and Wolf (2026)).
Why do monetary policy lags vary over time?
Different studies may find different policy lags in part because the speed of the
transmission mechanism varies over time. The first stage of monetary policy
transmission to financial market interest rates and broader financial conditions tends to
be quick. But lags in the second stage of transmission to demand and inflation are
longer and variable. They can differ depending on the state of the economy and the
shocks that monetary policy is responding to. Lags will be shorter if households and in
particular firms are forward-looking and anticipate the effects of monetary policy on
demand. Such expectations effects may be stronger when attentiveness to monetary
policy is high, which will tend to be the case when inflation is elevated and changes to
Bank Rate are frequent and large.
Staff analysis building on Di Pace et al (2025) finds that monetary policy can affect
inflation quickly via firms’ expectations. Firms’ own-price expectations in the DMP
survey adjust quickly to monetary policy news (Chart E.2). That quick response
appears to reflect in part changing expectations of demand owing to changes in
monetary policy, with sales expectations also responding quickly. But the analysis
indicates that expected prices move less than realised prices, suggesting that firms
may not fully anticipate the eventual impact of monetary policy on prices through
demand.

The firm expectations channel will tend to be stronger the more attentive firms are to
monetary policy. This channel may have contributed to the relatively fast policy
transmission during the 2021–23 tightening cycle documented in several studies (eg
Ascari et al (2026) and the Bank’s first Forecast Evaluation Report (2026)). Firms
may have paid particularly close attention to monetary policy over that period due the
size and speed of changes to inflation and Bank Rate. And changes in firms’ own-price
expectations in the DMP survey appear to have affected realised prices more quickly.
Because aggregate inflation was high, firms adjusted their own prices more frequently
to keep up with changes in input prices and competitors’ prices. For example, the
share of firms responding to the DMP survey who changed prices monthly rose from
22% in 2019 to 36% in 2022 (Bunn et al (2023)).

What would be the implications of faster transmission for setting monetary
policy?
Policy transmission is currently unlikely to be as fast as in 2021–23 but could still be
somewhat faster than on average in previous cycles. Inflation is projected to remain
well below its 2022 peak, but firms are still attentive to inflation and interest rates due
to the recent experience of high inflation (Box A). And while the frequency of price
setting has fallen back somewhat, it remains higher than before 2022.
A somewhat faster transmission mechanism could at the margin motivate a more
activist policy strategy. Long policy lags are one reason for looking through the direct,
and partially looking through the indirect, effects of the current energy shock on CPI
inflation (Box G in the April 2026 Monetary Policy Report). If lags are long, the
impact of tighter policy may peak after the energy shock has faded. If instead lags
were shorter, policy may be able to at least partly offset the near-term inflation impact
of the energy shock.
But while faster transmission would improve policymakers’ ability to stabilise inflation in
the near term, it does not remove the trade-off between the speed with which inflation
is returned to target and the consideration that should be placed on the variability of
output. UK monetary policy cannot affect global energy prices, so offsetting the direct
and indirect effects of higher energy prices would require pushing down domestic price
and wage inflation. This would create a larger output gap and an increase in
unemployment. It could also hinder the economy’s adaption to the higher relative price
of energy, delaying the efficient reallocation of resources across sectors and leading to
reductions in aggregate production (Box G in the April 2026 Monetary Policy
Report).
Shorter lags may generally suggest a more activist policy approach, but they may also
reduce the need to take pre-emptive action against the risk of large second-round
effects. Faster policy transmission could allow policymakers to wait longer for evidence
on second-round effects, as shorter lags would make it easier to squeeze out secondround effects once they appear. This approach could reduce the risk of overly tight
policy weighing on activity and pushing inflation below target in the medium term.
But when there remains uncertainty about the length of monetary policy lags, leaning
against the risk of second-round effects early may be a more robust strategy than
assuming that monetary policy could squeeze out second-round effects quickly after

Bank of England

Page 64

they arise. Policymakers must also consider to what extent the likelihood of large
second-round effects may depend on the conduct of monetary policy, including on the
near-term inflation outlook which monetary policy may be able to affect if lags are
short. Looking through direct effects, partially looking through indirect effects, and only
responding to second-round effects as they appear could raise the likelihood that large
second-round effects arise. To mitigate this risk, it may be appropriate to respond
quickly to rising risks of second-round effects, rather than wait for conclusive evidence,
even if lags were shorter.

3: Outlook, scenarios and risks
The UK economy continues to face high and volatile global energy prices resulting from
conflict in the Middle East; oil and gas prices have risen further in recent weeks. This will
lead to inflation rising further above the 2% target in the near term. But there is significant
uncertainty around the length of time energy prices will remain elevated, the extent to
which high energy prices will generate persistent inflationary pressures, and their impact
on demand.
This section presents a central projection that the majority of the MPC agree is a
reasonable baseline for the economic outlook, with energy prices conditioned on current
futures curves that decline over time. Conditioned on the market path for interest rates,
second-round effects are judged likely to occur in this projection, contributing to inflation
remaining above the 2% target over much of the forecast period, before declining below it
as the impact of rising energy prices on 12-month inflation reverses, and monetary policy
lowers inflation. Second-round effects are assumed to contribute, on average, a little over
0.5 percentage points to inflation over the second half of the forecast period. This is around
0.3 percentage points larger than the assessment in the July Report, reflecting the rise in
energy prices since then.
The central projection is complemented by two scenarios that explore some of the key
risks currently shaping the UK economic outlook. An ‘adverse inflation’ scenario assumes
that energy prices are persistently higher than in the central projection and that those are
associated with stronger second-round effects. As a result, CPI inflation is persistently
above the inflation target, conditioned on the market path for interest rates. A ‘weaker
demand’ scenario assumes that energy prices follow the path implied by futures prices and
explores the possibility that demand is materially less resilient to elevated energy prices.
Weaker demand increases the existing spare capacity in the economy and limits
inflationary pressure, with no second-round effects assumed to emerge.
Illustrative policy rules suggest that, in the central projection, a more restrictive monetary
policy stance is required than in July to return inflation to the 2% target. Policy rules
suggest that the adverse inflation scenario requires a more restrictive stance than the
central projection, partly reflecting greater second-round effects. The weaker demand
scenario requires a less restrictive stance, as inflation falls below target in the medium term
and spare capacity increases.

3.1 Key risks and uncertainties
Energy prices and second-round effects
The UK economy is facing a continued energy price shock, which has worsened since the
July Report as prices of oil, gas and many related products have risen materially (Section
1.1). While estimates suggest oil exports from the Middle East have recovered recently and
are close to the levels seen following the US-Iran ceasefire in June, a combination of other
factors has pushed up oil prices. These include higher transport costs for oil, a risk of
intensification of the conflict, and low global oil inventories. Higher oil prices have also pushed
up the prices of refined products, for example diesel, which continue to include an elevated
crack spread due to a reduction in Middle Eastern and Russian refining capacity. Gas prices
have risen by more than oil prices since the July Report (Chart 1.5). The evolution of all these
factors is uncertain: energy prices could rise if, for example, conflict in the Middle East
worsens but could fall faster than futures curves suggest, for example if there were to be a
durable resolution to the conflict.
The impact of higher energy prices on economic activity is uncertain. Higher energy prices
could weaken demand by undermining some of the factors that have supported the recent
resilience in activity. There is uncertainty around how long households can draw on savings or
save less to smooth through the hit to real incomes as energy prices feed through to higher
inflation, and to what extent companies can absorb higher costs before their financial
resources are depleted.
The impact of higher energy prices on UK inflation is also uncertain, particularly via indirect
and second-round effects. There is limited signal from the available data that second-round
effects have already started to emerge (Box A). But the MPC continues to judge that the risk
of these effects emerging is greater the longer the energy shock persists, for example as a
result of greater attentiveness by households and businesses to prices which leads to rising
inflation expectations.

Global goods prices
The outlook for non-energy import prices for the UK is another important source of uncertainty
about the UK inflation outlook at present. One important known factor is the current El Niño
weather event. Agricultural and commodity prices likely incorporate some anticipation of the
impact of El Niño, but its impact could prove larger than anticipated or captured by current
futures curves, particularly for global food prices (Box B). There is also uncertainty over the
outlook for Chinese export prices, which have historically been a source of disinflation for the
UK. Chinese export prices have risen over recent months, with increases in the prices of AIrelated exports the largest factor. There is a risk that Chinese export prices continue to rise,

Bank of England

Page 71

increasing external inflationary pressure for the UK economy. This risk is balanced by the
potential for trade diversion resulting from changes to global tariffs, which could lower
Chinese export prices to the UK (Box D of the July 2026 Monetary Policy Report).
There is also a risk of further global negative supply shocks, particularly given their increased
frequency in recent years. One pertinent example, which could amplify the effects of El Niño,
is a disruption to fertiliser supply. Global fertiliser prices remain above levels typical of the
2010s, although well below the peaks reached in 2022. Disruptions to fertiliser supply, for
example if some countries were to impose export restrictions, could raise food prices, with
effects that could be persistent. There also remains a risk of more general disruptions to
supply chains given broader geopolitical risks, which would also put upward pressure on CPI
inflation.

Monetary policy restrictiveness
Although assessments of the degree to which the stance of monetary policy is restrictive or
accommodative to the economy vary, overall evidence is consistent with the current stance of
monetary policy being restrictive.
Nevertheless, there are several significant uncertainties over the current impact monetary
policy is having on the economy. One example is the speed at which past changes in Bank
Rate are feeding through to inflation (Box E). If the impact of monetary policy on the economy
has been faster than historical norms, increases in Bank Rate between 2021–23 may now be
providing less of a drag than some models suggest, which could explain some of the recent
resilience in GDP growth.
Another important uncertainty is around the extent to which increases in the market curve for
interest rates have resulted in a more restrictive stance of monetary policy. Nominal interest
rates have risen, which is feeding through into nominal lending costs. This impact may be
larger for households than for some businesses given that household bank lending rates tend
to be linked to the market curve whereas corporate bank lending rates often reference Bank
Rate.
However, nominal interest rates and measures adjusted for expected inflation – real financial
conditions – can give different signals (Mann (2026)). Some measures of real rate gaps, for
example those derived from the Bank’s Market Participants Survey, suggest smaller changes
in real financial conditions than changes in nominal conditions. These measures are highly
uncertain in part because the equilibrium real interest rate r* – the time-varying real interest
rate that, if the economy started from a position of no output gap and inflation at target, would
sustain output at potential and inflation at target – is not observable and estimates vary.

3.2: Central projection and scenarios
To help explore the span of some of the key risks, the MPC considers a number of projections
for the economic outlook. The central projection is based on a staff proposal that the majority
of the MPC agrees is a reasonable baseline. This section also sets out two complementary
scenarios: an ‘adverse inflation’ scenario and a ‘weaker demand’ scenario. These explore
impacts of plausible alternative assumptions for energy prices, demand growth and secondround effects (Table 3.A and Chart 3.1). The central projection and scenarios collectively in
this Report represent a broader range of outcomes than the scenarios published in the July
Report.
The adverse inflation scenario explores the risk of higher energy prices and the higher
second-round effects which might occur as a consequence. The path for energy prices
assumed in this scenario is higher than in the analogous adverse scenario in July. This
reflects the rise in global energy prices since the July Report and is consistent with persistent
disruption to global energy markets.
The weaker demand scenario assumes the same path for future energy prices as the central
projection but assumes that GDP growth slows materially and that widening spare capacity in
the economy reduces the scope for businesses to raise prices or grant higher pay, such that
no second-round effects emerge. This could be consistent with the lack of evidence for
second-round effects currently.

unemployment rate to 5.1%, and there is no widespread labour market shakeout (Chart 3.4,

Adverse inflation scenario
In the adverse inflation scenario, energy prices rise materially above the paths implied by
current futures curves and remain persistently elevated (Chart 3.1). This leads to materially
higher inflation in the near term, primarily through the direct and indirect effects of energy
prices. Further ahead, strong second-round effects keep inflation persistently above the 2%
target, conditioned on market interest rates (Chart 3.3). Direct and indirect effects push up
already elevated longer-term inflation expectations and lead to faster nominal wage growth
and larger pass-through to firms’ pricing. The second-round effects in this scenario operate
through the same channels as in the adverse scenario in the July Report but are larger owing
to the higher path for energy prices.
Higher energy prices also reduce GDP growth in the scenario. Energy prices tend to affect
demand by more than supply. As a result, there is somewhat more spare capacity in the
economy over the forecast period than in the central projection. But this additional spare
capacity is assumed not to be sufficient to materially constrain workers’ wage bargaining
power or firms’ ability to raise prices.

Weaker demand scenario
In the weaker demand scenario, energy prices follow the same futures curve as in the central
projection. The key difference in this scenario is that domestic demand weakens rapidly, so
activity is lower than in the central projection. This could occur if households are unable or
unwilling to smooth consumption through the real income shock from high energy prices, for
example if some households exhaust savings buffers that they have been drawing down to
sustain consumption. Or businesses that initially absorbed higher costs may become less
able to do so as their financial resources are depleted and as the shock proves more
persistent, leading to increased insolvencies, lower employment and weaker investment.
Weaker demand results in materially more spare capacity in the economy than in the central
projection.
Inflation is lower in the weaker demand scenario than in the central projection. Greater spare
capacity limits wage and price pressures, such that no second-round effects emerge.
Conditioned on the market path for interest rates, inflation falls below the 2% target during
2028 and reaches 1.2% in 2029.

3.3: Central projection and scenarios under alternative monetary
policy stances
The central projection and scenarios described above are conditioned on the market curve for
interest rates. If the assumptions underlying any particular projection or scenario came to
pass, the appropriate stance of policy would likely vary from that market path.
Illustrative policy rules can provide information about the effects of alternative monetary policy
stances for different scenarios. These rules inform the MPC’s assessment, but do not
translate mechanically into Bank Rate decisions. Two such policy rules are shown below. One
is a contemporaneous Taylor-type rule incorporating the output gap and non-energy inflation.
This rule helps to demonstrate a policy path that ‘looks through’ the direct effects of an energy
shock, while responding to indirect and second-round effects, which may be informative in the
current economic environment. The other is an illustrative model-based projection, which
constructs policy paths to minimise a loss function intended to capture policymakers’
preferences over macroeconomic outcomes, including deviations of overall inflation from
target, variation in the output gap and changes in interest rates.
The market curve for interest rates includes expectations for future Bank Rate and risk premia
(Lengyel and Walker (2026)). The policy rules presented here should be interpreted as
exploring alternative overall policy stances, comparable to the stance implied by the market
curve, rather than a path for future Bank Rate decisions.

Bank of England

Illustrative policy rules suggest a more restrictive stance for monetary policy is
required than in July to return inflation to the 2% target in the central projection.
For the central projection, taken together the two illustrative policy paths are broadly similar to
the market curve in the near term, but somewhat lower further out (Chart 3.5). On average,
they point to a somewhat looser overall policy stance than implied by the market curve. The
non-energy Taylor-type rule is slower to respond than the illustrative model-based projection,
reflecting the fact that it excludes the direct effects of energy price changes which account for
much of the near-term rise in inflation, and under that stance inflation does not return to the
2% target within the forecast period.
The paths implied by illustrative policy rules vary but have risen since the July Report. The
rules associated with the central projection have risen by between 0.6–0.9 percentage points
on average over 2027–29, given higher energy prices and the associated higher secondround effects. In part reflecting market participants’ assessment of the impact of the economic
news since the July Report, the market curve has risen by around 0.6 percentage points on
average over 2027–29.

Annex 2: Monetary policy since the July 2026 Report
At its meeting ending on 16 September 2026, the Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) voted to
maintain Bank Rate at 3.75%.
Protracted conflict in the Middle East had contributed to further increases in crude and refined
energy prices since the previous meeting, which remained more volatile and higher than preconflict. UK CPI inflation had increased to 3.1% in August and was likely to rise further over
coming quarters. Monetary policy was being set to ensure inflation came down to 2%
sustainably as the economy adjusts to the energy shock. The policy stance required to
achieve this would depend on the scale and duration of the shock and how it propagated
through the economy.
There had been little evidence of material second-round effects in price and wage-setting.
However, the risk of such effects, against which policy needed to lean, was greater the longer
higher energy prices persist or were more volatile. Activity had been slightly stronger than
expected, although soft labour market conditions, and the higher interest rates faced by
households and businesses since the conflict began, would act to reduce inflation over time.
Overall, the Committee judged that the risks to the inflation outlook were tilted to the upside,
and more so than at the time of the July Monetary Policy Report, although there remained
scope for the outlook to change materially as events in the Middle East unfolded.
The Committee judged that it was appropriate to maintain Bank Rate at its September
meeting. The Committee stood ready to act as necessary to ensure that CPI inflation
remained on track to meet the 2% target in the medium term.
At this meeting, the MPC also voted unanimously to reduce the stock of UK government bond
purchases held for monetary policy purposes, and financed by the issuance of central bank
reserves, to zero. This would be conducted through a multi-year plan, such that the remaining
stock would be unwound at an annual average pace of £46 billion by the end of 2034, through
annual sales of £20 billion alongside maturing gilts.
