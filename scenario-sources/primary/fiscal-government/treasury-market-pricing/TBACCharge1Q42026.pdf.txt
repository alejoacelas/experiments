Executive summary
We observe a large tech financing cycle, but no clear evidence of broad crowding out to date.
Higher Treasury yields primarily reflect growth, policy expectations and energy, not an
issuance-driven supply shock.

Financing needs are large and broadening.

Nearly $600 billion of tech-related debt is projected in
2026 and more than $700 billion in 2027, with upside risk
and a growing role for equity.

Tech borrowers retain capacity and incentive to invest.

Aggregate net leverage for the tech ecosystem is 0.28x
versus 1.3x for the S&P excluding financials; expected
returns support continued spending, although individual
borrowers remain vulnerable.

Capital is becoming selective, not broadly unavailable.

Lenders are demanding stronger terms and secured
structures within tech; Treasury auction results and
resilient non-tech spreads show no clear evidence of
broader crowding out.

4

The investor base can expand.

Insurers, pensions, households and foreign investors
provide multiple sources of demand; modest reallocations
from cash and equities could finance a meaningful share
of projected borrowing.

5

Capex-driven growth has been the principal transmission
channel to rates, not overwhelming supply.

Growth, policy expectations and energy best explain higher
Treasury yields; the analysis finds little independent
explanatory power from tech issuance.

Conclusions
The capex financing cycle is large, accelerating, and broadening. Tech-related debt issuance is projected to rise from
approximately $586 billion in 2026 to $735 billion in 2027, with upside risk and an increasing role for secured
borrowing and equity. Intentions to raise excess capital above projected funding gaps may suggest concern over
eventual capital scarcity.
The technology ecosystem retains both the capacity and the incentive to invest. Low aggregate leverage and
expected returns above the cost of capital support continued borrowing and spending, but the strength of the overall
cohort should not obscure the vulnerabilities of individual borrowers.
Capital provisioning is becoming more discerning, not yet broadly unavailable. Lenders are demanding more
favorable terms and secured structures, while lower-rated borrowers face greater scrutiny; crowding out is most
evident within the technology ecosystem rather than across the broader Fixed Income markets.
The investor base has capacity to finance the next phase. Insurers, pensions, households, and foreign investors
provide multiple sources of demand, while even modest reallocations from cash and equities into fixed income could
finance a meaningful share of projected borrowing.
There is no clear evidence to date that tech issuance is crowding out Treasuries or non-tech credit. Treasury
auction results and resilient non-tech credit spreads suggest that markets are absorbing the financing wave, with
supply-related repricing concentrated within the technology ecosystem rather than impairing access to capital more
broadly.
Higher Treasury yields are primarily a growth, policy-expectations, and energy story, not an issuance-driven
supply shock. The analysis finds little independent explanatory power from tech issuance; the durability of capex-led
growth remains more important for the rates outlook than financing volumes alone.
