Treasury Derivatives
Treasury Borrowing Advisory Committee
November 4th, 2026

Please comment on the growth in the use of Treasury derivatives, in particular swaps and futures.
How has use of these derivatives evolved?
Who are the primary users and what factors have driven their usage?
How do these derivatives affect demand for cash Treasury securities?
What effects do these derivatives have on Treasury repo markets?
Does the use of derivatives by investors reduce the size of the Treasury repo market?
Should Treasury consider any policy initiatives to increase demand for cash Treasury securities relative to Treasury derivatives?

1

Executive summary
Since the 2022 rise in yields, asset managers have built synthetic Treasury exposure through futures, and LDI investors (pension funds and
insurers) have used rate swaps for duration, while heavy government and credit issuance have weighed on cash Treasuries.
The reasons for expressing duration through derivatives are many:
• Futures bring simplicity in execution (no repo infrastructure or counterparty lines). They are a versatile and liquid duration management tool
(weekly volume is 1.5-2x the open interest and comparable to cash Treasury volume), anonymous (all-to-all central limit order books) and low
maintenance (one roll a quarter) for deploying U.S. duration. Their usage leaves cash available for higher-yielding assets and avoids higher fund
expense ratios (repo interest is booked as an expense).
• Swaps have been standardized in execution and clearing since the 2013/2014 clearing/SEF mandates but remain customizable (matched to
liability cashflows, low initial margin, can extend beyond 30y), carry no roll, can be collateralized with securities, and hedge liabilities that are
themselves discounted on swap curves.
• Historically, dealers would intermediate these positions directly. However, leverage ratio rules and balance-sheet constraints have led to reduced
participation from that cohort and that gap has been filled by hedge funds.
Derivatives do not remove the underlying demand for Treasuries, they change who ends up holding them. They also broaden market access and
add intermediation capacity.
Use of derivatives increases the size of repo market. Also, the availability of repo affects the pricing of derivatives which in turn influences the size of
the derivatives market. The cash demand derivatives generate through leveraged, repo-funded positions is pro-cyclical and weakens when funding
or margin tightens.
Policies that curb derivatives risk disrupting today’s market structure. Treasury could consider policy initiatives that make cash Treasuries easier to
hold and finance relative to derivatives: easing regulation that restricts balance sheet usage, making repo financing easier to access, improving the
availability of term repo financing, opening repo market making to new entrants.
Together, these initiatives could help build a more diversified and robust base of cash Treasury holders and keep Treasury demand steady.

Usage of U.S. rates derivatives and evolution over time
1980s-2007: Pre-Great Financial Crisis, dealer-centric and over-the-counter (OTC for short) markets
• Swaps were a dealer product sold to banks (Asset and Liability Management or ALM for short) and corporates (fixed to floating). End users mostly
received fixed while dealers paid fixed, hedged in cash Treasuries on repo and warehoused the residual. This flow, combined with LIBOR credit risk,
kept swap spreads positive.
• Inter-dealer trades peaked at 66% of OTC IRD (Interest Rate Derivatives) turnover in 2001 (Bank for International Settlements or BIS for short)2.
• Hedge funds were present at the long end as spread convergence traders (see Long-Term Capital Management and swap spreads in 1998).
2008-2015: Post-Great Financial Crisis, displacement of inter-dealer markets by buy-side participants
• Inter-dealer trade share dropped to 44% in 2010 and 35% in 2013 (BIS)2.
• Issuer swap demand faded with lower rates and pension funds and insurers (LDI for short) became the structural receivers of fixed. The 30y swap
spread turned negative in 2008.
• Clearing/SEF mandates in 2013-14 standardized swap markets.
• GSE (Government-Sponsored Enterprises) swap usage shrank after conservatorship, removing a major structural payer of fixed (synthetic fixed-rate
funding and convexity hedging of retained Mortgage-Backed Security - or MBS for short - portfolios).
2015-2020: Regulations, balance sheet constraints and dealer wind-down of warehousing
• Negative swap spreads and leverage ratio regulation pushed warehousing from dealers to hedge funds. The basis trade picked up before seeing a
large unwind in March 2020 (Covid).
2020-2026: Asset managers and leveraged funds/dealers mirrored positioning
• Asset managers’ futures long have roughly quadrupled since the end of 2021 and the leveraged funds (and dealer) community has seen a close to
identical increase in their shorts (according to the Commodity Futures Trading Commission Traders in Financial Futures data - or CFTC TFF for
short).

Growth and evolution of U.S. rates derivatives usage (futures and swaps)
The open interest in Treasury futures has increased in line with the
stock of U.S. public debt/marketable (share hovering between 12%
and 25%, peaking right before the deleveraging of March 2020).
It has recently stabilized at around 18% of the stock of coupons.
Swaps open interest used to be much larger than the stock of debt
and that ratio has compressed from ~18x coupons ex System
Open Market Account (or SOMA for short) in 2014 to ~7x (CFTC
Weekly Swaps Report).
Gross USD swap notional has ranged between $85tn and $135tn
for about a decade (compression, then the SOFR conversion kept
notional down) while coupons ex SOMA grew 2.5x, so the falling
ratio reflects notional efficiency and fiscal expansion and QT rather
than a retreat from swaps.
In terms of volumes, cash Treasury and futures volumes on a 13week moving-average basis are similar at above $4tn a week.
Swap volumes have run higher, at $6-7tn a week on the same
basis, with the latest week above $8tn (see figure on slide 17).

USD swaps open interest and counterparty composition
As mentioned in slide 4, two episodes reduced outstanding notional in swaps without impacting activity:
• Compression took the open interest down 37% in 2014-16 as the leverage ratio and G-SIB (Global Systemically Important Bank) surcharge (both
keyed to gross notional) made redundant line items a capital cost.
• The SOFR transition then replaced LIBOR fixed-float swaps with SOFR OIS, now ~$100tn outstanding.
Since 2025 notional has grown again, with OIS up $27tn, putting the total back around its 2014 level.
The non-dealer share of outstanding USD interest rate swap counterparty sides has roughly doubled, from about 15% in 2014 to about 31% in 2026
(CFTC Weekly Swaps Report participant tables). The remaining two-thirds are swap-dealer sides, which include inter-dealer trades and dealers’ own
hedges. The CFTC reporting-method changes in 2019 and 2022 each reset the level lower (indicated on the chart), so the trend within each regime is the
more useful signal to interpret this data.

Primary users of Treasury futures
Consistent with a previous TBAC charge (TBAC Charge, Q1 20241),
the CFTC data show that since 2015 (with a near-full unwind
between 2020 and late 2021 after rates bottomed), asset managers
have built a large net long position in Treasury futures of ~$1.15tn
notional (peaking at ~$1.4tn in February 2026).
In lockstep, leveraged funds, followed by dealers more recently
(coinciding with de-regulation developments such as the SLR
(Supplementary Leverage Ratio) relaxation in 2020-21 and eSLR
reform of 2026), have taken a mirrored position in short futures.
Other cohorts have maintained negligible positions throughout the
period.
Since the end of 2022, hedge-fund repo borrowing is up $1.9tn
(Form PF)6, leveraged funds’ net short Treasury futures $0.7tn
(CFTC) and MMF (Money Market Funds) lending through FICC
sponsored repo $1.0tn (OFR). These three series moving together
since 2018 are evidence of the basis trade financing chain.
The size of the basis trade is estimated at $0.8-1.2tn depending on
the methodology used (see slide 15)6,22.

Primary users of interest rate swaps
Insurers (U.S. life companies)

Pension funds/LDI

• The latest published aggregates (National Association of
Insurance Commissioners or NAIC for YE202111, S&P
Global Market Intelligence for YE202313) put U.S. insurers’
derivatives at about $3tn of notional, half of it swaps across
all asset classes. 95% of the total is designated as hedging
and 98% is held by life companies. The latest NAIC industry
report covers YE2021.

• U.S. corporate defined benefit (DB) plans discount liabilities on AA corporate yields
(ASC 715), so their LDI is largely physical long credit and STRIPS, with swaps and
futures in completion overlays. There is no U.S. aggregate of their derivatives. Public
plans discount at expected return (GASB 67/68) and hedge little.

• Interest rate swaps have been the largest swap type in
every NAIC report we reviewed since 2010: $911bn at
YE201712 (80% of swap notional) and $1.12tn at YE2021
(73%)11, with life companies alone at $1.16tn by Q4 2022
(Risk.net, Q4 2022)14. On the data released, the book
roughly tripled from 2010 to 2018 and has grown more
slowly since, to $1.1-1.2tn.

• Where we can observe direction, it is one-way: UK LDI and pension funds held £167bn
net receive-fixed interest rate swaps and £57bn inflation swaps in September 202215.
More than 70% of Dutch pension funds hedge liabilities as net fixed receivers in longdated swaps16, because the FTK (Dutch regulatory financial assessment framework)
values liabilities off the swap curve. De Nederlandsche Bank (DNB) and market
participants expect the Dutch pension reform (2026-28) to reduce that long-end
receive demand.

• The swaps lengthen asset duration against annuity and
other long-dated liabilities while the assets stay in credit.
NAIC’s published aggregates do not give direction, but the
liability structure makes insurers receivers of fixed, and the
one filings-based aggregation that reports it (Risk.net, Q4
2022) finds just over half the book received-fixed.
• Futures are marginal (5.8% of notional, NAIC YE2021).

Primary users of interest rate swaps (continued)
Negative swap spreads and hedge fund positioning
• A cleared swap costs a bank very little in leverage ratio terms
while a repo-financed Treasury position costs capital. Swap rates
are therefore pushed below Treasury yields, most of all at the
long end, where receiving demand is deepest and duration supply
heaviest. The 30y spread has averaged -73bp this year and
touched -97bp on 8 April 2025, the widest since SOFR spreads
began trading in 2018. At the front end, where futures carry little
delivery optionality, the same balance-sheet cost shows up in the
futures basis: the 2y swap spread and TU futures richness have
moved together since 2018 (see chart at top right)
• Hedge funds take the other side (long cash Treasuries on repo,
pay fixed). The Fed’s Form PF decomposition (Monin, 2026)6
puts the swap-spread trade at $305bn in September 2025 (see
chart at bottom right), which is about ten times its end-2022 size.
The BIS’s upper-bound method gives $631bn at Q2 2025 and
when applied to the Fed’s September 2025 aggregates it gives
about $750bn (our calculation).
• Like the basis trade, it is a levered short on the dealer balancesheet constraint, which is why it loses and de-levers when that
constraint binds (March 2020 and April 2025, when the BIS
measure contracted 11% between March and June 2025).

Why do users gravitate towards derivatives?
Operational hurdles: asset managers, corporates, pensions
• Levering Treasuries in cash form requires financing them in repo, which means having a functional repo desk, counterparty lines and
documentation, collateral management and settlement infrastructure.
• That set-up cost is borne regardless of position size. Derivatives deliver the equivalent exposure without these hurdles.
• Futures are the lightest route: one exchange-traded contract per curve point, central clearing, anonymity, no documentation beyond a futures
commission merchant (FCM for short). Asset managers and anyone without a financing desk start here.
• Similarly, swaps are standardized from a product standpoint yet customizable in their details and can match liabilities precisely.
• While not as demanding as setting up repo trading infrastructure, the set-up required to trade swaps is heavier, involving an ISDA
(International Swaps and Derivatives Association) agreement, clearing-broker relationships, swap trading platforms and collateral operations.
Pensions, insurers, GSEs and corporates accept that cost because only a swap can be matched to a liability or a bond issue, which a future
cannot.
Funding certainty: asset managers, pensions, insurers
• Repo-financed Treasuries must be rolled continuously (mostly overnight to one week), exposing the holder to rate and availability risk at
every roll date.
• Derivatives embed the availability of term financing (but not its rate): a future locks it to the delivery date at the implied repo rate (three
months, rolled quarterly) while a swap funds at SOFR for the life of the trade with no roll required, but at whatever rate SOFR prints. Bilateral
swaps can be collateralized with securities rather than daily cash.
• Someone still has to carry the cash bond and roll the repo. That is the role hedge funds and dealers play through the basis and swap-spread
trades: they have the infrastructure and provide the term financing the end users have outsourced.
12

Why do users gravitate towards derivatives? (continued)
Classification bias: asset managers, insurers, GSEs, corporates
• Fund accounting treats repo interest as an explicit expense: it raises the reported expense ratio and reduces net investment income
available for distribution, both of which matter to the end investor and therefore the managers.
• The identical financing cost inside a future or swap is netted within the instrument’s price and never appears as a fund expense.
Economically equivalent leverage is therefore presented asymmetrically, biasing fund managers toward derivatives.
• Section 18 of the 1940 Act caps fund borrowing, including repo, at one-third of assets, while under SEC Rule 18f-4 (2020) derivatives are
governed by a VaR limit that permits far more exposure.
• Hedge accounting pulls the liability hedgers towards swaps: a swap whose terms match the debt or liability qualifies cleanly under ASC 815
(FASB), IFRS 9 (IASB) or SSAP 86 (NAIC), whereas a futures hedge must be re-designated at every roll and tested for effectiveness each
period.

Tax implications: tax-exempt investors, taxable individuals
• For tax-exempt investors such as pensions, endowments and foundations, financing cash Treasuries with repo makes the bonds' income
partly taxable as unrelated business taxable income (UBTI) and requires a Form 990-T filing. Treasury futures and swaps give the same
leveraged duration without borrowing, so their gains generally stay tax-exempt. This pushes these investors toward futures rather than
owning cash Treasuries on repo, unless they invest through a corporate blocker such as a mutual fund or offshore feeder fund.
• For taxable U.S. investors, futures gains are 60/40 long- and short-term capital gains under U.S. Internal Revenue Code Section 1256 while
repo interest is an ordinary deduction limited by Section 163(d) (the same leverage is cheaper after tax in futures).

Futures basis
• Futures have traded fair to slightly rich to the replicating cash-plus-repo position for most of the post-2008
period21. Over the last two years, the implied repo on TU, FV, TY and UXY ran roughly 0-15bp above matched
SOFR OIS, highest in TU and FV (Morgan Stanley, Sept. 2026)22.
• Net basis equals gross basis less carry to the assumed delivery date, where carry reflects coupon and accrued
interest income less the cost of funding the cheapest-to-deliver (CTD) issue in the term repo market.
• Term repo generally trades at a 5-15bp premium (higher in rate) to SOFR OIS to the same date. In addition,
CTDs trade special occasionally, which benefits basis-trade holders by lowering funding costs.
• As a result, futures that screen fair to term repo carry a positive margin when financing is rolled overnight.
• Hedge-fund basis positions are financed through a mix of overnight cleared repo and term uncleared repo. The
positive carry pick-up applies to the overnight-financed portion, in exchange for daily roll risk.
• Per OFR Brief 26-01²⁰, about one-third of the $2.4tn hedge fund repo book (~$800bn as of Q3 2024) is cleared
through FICC sponsored repo, which is mostly overnight. The rest is largely uncleared bilateral repo, which runs
at longer tenors.
• Recent spikes in US and WN net basis reflect embedded optionality and reveal less about implied funding
conditions.

End-user derivative exposure creates cash Treasury and repo demand through intermediaries
Derivatives broaden and deepen demand for cash treasuries –
• by expanding market access: lower operational barriers have
doubled the number of reportable traders in TU/FV/TY since 2008
(CFTC, TFF)
• by providing capital efficient access to leverage.
• by adding hedging capacity that helps the market absorb and
redistribute issuance.
Long derivative exposure increases repo demand through leveraged
cash intermediation, which:
• creates pro-cyclical intermediation risk​.
• concentrates cash demand among hedge funds and, increasingly,
dealers, who offset about half of each week’s change in their cash
Treasury inventory with futures
• weakens when funding or margin tightens.
• can unwind precisely when the cash market needs buyers (March
2020).
• requires wider basis and swap spreads to compensate
intermediaries for scarce balance sheet and repo capacity.
Derivatives increase market access and intermediation capacity, in
the process reshape who holds treasuries. The associated cash
Treasury demand becomes less reliable when leverage is scarce.
16

Investors' derivative longs become hedge-fund and dealer cash Treasuries financed in repo
Indirectly through hedge funds and dealers, the usage of derivatives
for leverage expands the demand for cash Treasuries as it gives
access to Treasury exposure for investor cohorts that might
otherwise be unable to participate directly (by lowering operational
barriers and enabling efficient leverage).
An increase in long derivative positions by the structural participants
therefore raises repo usage, since hedge funds intermediate by
buying Treasuries on a levered basis. That intermediation rests
mainly with hedge funds, with dealers stepping in more recently as
balance-sheet constraints eased.
Importantly, a reduction in the structural longs (a return of rates
towards zero, as in 2020-21, could be one trigger) could lead to an
unwind of the basis and swap-spread trades and the paired long
Treasury holdings of hedge funds and dealers.
Derivatives pricing is tied to repo availability: scarcer or costlier repo
means hedge funds and dealers demand more to provide liquidity,
widening the basis and swap spreads. Conversely, easier repo
compresses them.

Policy initiatives: strengthen cash Treasuries through repo​
Policies that curb derivatives risk disrupting today’s market structure. A more durable approach is to make cash Treasury financing broader, cheaper
and more reliable.
Robust repo access reinforces what makes Treasuries unique: depth,
liquidity, resilience and global benchmark status
• Broaden repo participation​ by incentivizing institutions to build repo
infrastructure.
• Encourage new intermediaries that can aggregate and consolidate repo
flows and offer repo services to end users that lack direct market access.
• Expand access to the Fed’s Standing Overnight Repo Operations (SRP)
beyond primary dealers and banks.
• Preserve seven-day put structures in sponsored repo to maintain MMF
participation in term financing.
• Assess potential trade-offs between higher MMF liquidity buffers and term
repo market depth.
• Develop deeper term liquidity in cleared repo.
Reduce operational frictions associated with Treasury settlement
• Streamline Fedwire settlement, fails management and intraday liquidity
processes to lower participant burden.
• Improve matching of receives and deliveries to reduce operational and
funding costs.
• Automate pair-off and exception management to cut the manual resources
required.

Reduce balance-sheet constraints​
• Exempt Treasuries from the supplementary leverage ratio and re-evaluate
the Tier 1 leverage ratio.
• Recognize pre-positioned discount-window collateral in liquidity metrics,
freeing capacity for Treasury holdings and intermediation.
Reduce onboarding friction through greater standardization of repo and
clearing documentation
• Consolidate the multiple legal agreements needed before market
participation into a single, streamlined framework.
• Standardize terms on industry templates to limit bespoke negotiation.
• Shorten and standardize onboarding to encourage participation in cash
Treasury and repo markets.
Leveling the playing field between repo and derivatives
• Fund expenses: Exempt interest on Treasury-collateralized repo from the
operating expense ratio, with a separate disclosure of financing costs, so
that levered cash Treasury positions aren't penalized relative to futures.
• Tax exempt investors: Clarify or, if necessary, amend the tax rules so that
short-term, cleared Treasury repo doesn't create taxable income for taxexempt investors.
• Registered funds: Raise awareness of the Rule 18f-4 option to treat repo
as a derivative, with risk limits and disclosure.
