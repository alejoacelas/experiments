Household Growth to Increasingly Feel the Impacts of the Immigration Drop | Joint Center for Housing Studies
Tuesday, August 11, 2026
Author: Daniel McCue, Senior Research Associate

The sharp drop in immigration that began in 2025 contributed only modestly to the slowing of household growth in that year, but, according to our new analysis, the effects will likely be larger in 2026 and 2027. Examining historical data, we find that while many immigrants form households in their year of arrival, most of their net household formation occurs one to two years after they enter the country. This suggests that the impact of the 2025 immigration downturn on household growth will become more pronounced in 2026 and 2027, especially if immigration declines further as projected. Rough estimates indicate that the impact will grow from about 75,000 fewer households formed by immigrants in 2025 to nearly 500,000 fewer by 2027.

Timing of Household Formation by Recent Immigrants

According to the Census Bureau, after surging to 2.7 million in 2024, net international immigration dropped 54 percent in 2025, to 1.3 million people, and is projected to plummet another 75 percent in 2026 to just 0.3 million. Unsurprisingly, household growth slowed in 2025 as well, but more modestly than the decline in immigration, coming in 16 percent lower than in the previous year according to the Housing Vacancy Survey (HVS).

Digging into the Data

Using 2024 ACS data on immigrant-headed households by year of arrival, we estimate that of the 702,000 net increase in immigrant households that year, about 250,000 (36 percent) were headed by immigrants who arrived in 2024, 290,000 (41 percent) by those who arrived in 2023, and 102,000 (15 percent) by those who arrived in 2022 (Figure 1).

A quick analysis corroborates these findings and offers a rough estimate of the size of the expected impacts on household growth in 2025, 2026, and 2027. Applying updated immigration assumptions to historical household formation rates, we find that the decline in immigration in 2025 likely reduced household growth from recent immigrants in that year by about 75,000 households to around 567,000 (Figure 3). Household growth from recent immigrants declines an additional 237,000 households in 2026, to 330,000, after factoring in the projected additional decline in net immigration in 2026. And if immigration remains at 0.3 million in 2027, our estimates suggest household growth from recent immigrants in that year would decline another 187,000 households, to 143,000.

Note: Projected values based on JCHS estimates and net immigration assumptions of 1.3 million in 2025, and 0.3 million in 2026 and 2027. Source: JCHS tabulations of US Census Bureau, American Community Survey 1-Year Estimates and Population Estimates Program

Taken together, these estimates suggest that current immigration cutbacks are on track to lower the pace of household growth from recent immigrants by another 420,000 households per year in 2026 and 2027. This would bring the total decline between 2024 and 2027 to nearly 500,000 households per year. Given that the HVS reports that the total number of households increased by 1.1 million in 2025, this expected slowdown in growth from recent immigrants by itself would pull down overall household growth in 2027 to a level close to the Center’s low-series projection of 690,000 additional households per year over 2025–2035. This is absent any change in native-born household growth which is also slowing due to structural, long-term factors as documented in our most recent household projections. A slowdown of this magnitude would mark a significant shift in near-term household formation trends with broad implications for housing markets.

Projections of Homeownership Rates and Household Growth by Tenure for 2025–2035
Addendum: Tenure Projections for 2025–2035 under the Census Bureau’s Low-Immigration Scenario for Future Population Growth
Daniel McCue, Chris Herbert, and Riordan Frost December 2025

The Census Bureau’s low-immigration series population projection assumes net gains from international immigration in 2025–2035 of 420,000 per year, which is approximately half of the 870,000 assumed in the middle-series projection. Gains from natural growth are largely unchanged between the low- and middle-series projections.
Due to the decline in projected population growth, total household growth in the low immigration series projection is 20 percent lower than in the main series, slowing to 6.9 million in 2025–2035, down from the 8.6 million in the middle-series projection (Figure A2).

The majority (1.0 million) of the 1.7 million difference in household growth between the main and the low immigration projections is due to less growth in households under age 45. Meanwhile, projected growth in households age 65 and over dips by just 200,000 between the two series (from a gain of 7.9 million to a gain of 7.7 million).
