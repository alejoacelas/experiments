Each year, Common App releases an ongoing
series of deadline update research briefs to share
detailed and timely insights about the state of
first-year college applications and year-over-year
trends through a specific point in the application
season — in this case, November 1. We time
these briefs to capture activity for major college
application deadlines on the first of each month
from November to March.

* Corresponding author(s)

Press inquiries
Jackson Sternberg
jackson.sternberg@commonapp.org

By analyzing up-to-date application activity, we
bring attention to trends in applicant
race/ethnicity, socioeconomic status,
geographic residence, and the types of
institutions to which students apply. We hope to
empower enrollment leaders, counselors, and
other stakeholders with these insights as we
strive together to increase the accessibility of
the college admissions process in alignment
with our Next Chapter.
Notes: As Common App membership has grown over time, we
focus deadline updates on institutions that have maintained
Common App membership for the five most recent years
(“returning members”), or 965 institutions. That said, trends
observed here may still partially result from new members
bringing new applicants onto the platform each year. We also
note that some institutions that previously had an application
deadline on November 1 moved that deadline to later in the
month during the 2026-27 application cycle. Deadline changes
like these could account for variation in application volume
compared to prior years.

Key findings
1.​ Applicant and application counts grew; account creators decreased:
Through November 1, 2026, 992,093 distinct first-year applicants had
applied to 965 returning members, a slight increase (1%) from 981,292 at this
point in 2025–26. Conversely, account creators intending to enroll in college
the following year decreased by 2%, to 1,980,780, from a peak of 2,012,437
at this point in 2025–26.
a.​ Application volume to returning members through November 1 rose
6% from 2025–26 (4,980,983) to 2026–27 (5,301,045). Applicants
had also applied to more members through November 1 in 2026–27
than in 2025–26 (up 5% from 5.08 to 5.34 applications per applicant).
2.​ American Indian or Alaska Native applicants and Asian applicants were the
fastest-growing groups by race or ethnicity: American Indian or Alaska
Native applicants and Asian applicants grew by 9% and 3% respectively;
applicants identifying as an underrepresented minority race/ethnicity1 (URM)
increased by 1% in 2026–27.
3.​ Fee waiver eligible applicant growth outpaced their non-fee waiver
eligible peers: The number of applicants reporting eligibility for a Common
App fee waiver grew at a faster annual rate (+4%) than the count of ineligible
applicants (+1%) through this point in the cycle.
4.​ The Western region was the fastest growing region among domestic
applicants: The Western region grew significantly faster (6%) than the
next-highest Mid-Atlantic (1%) region, compared to a 3% decrease in the
New England region. The increase in Western applicants is driven by
substantial growth in Arizona, at 80% growth since this time last year.
5.​ The number of international applicants increased compared to this point
during the prior season after declining in 2025-26: While domestic
applicants remained flat compared to the prior season, the number of
international applicants increased by 9% (compared to a 9% year-over-year
decrease in applicants at this time during 2025–26). This growth was driven
in part by a pronounced increase in applicants from Europe (15%).

1

We use the term underrepresented minority (URM) in alignment with conventions
employed by the National Science Foundation. In this report, applicants identifying as Black
or African American, Latinx, American Indian or Alaska Native, or Native Hawaiian or Other
Pacific Islander are classified as URM applicants.

6.​ The count of applicants reporting test scores dropped, while the count of
applicants not reporting test scores increased: The number of applicants
reporting a test score decreased by 2% compared to this time during the
2025–26 reporting cycle, while the number of applicants who did not report
a test score increased by 5%.
7.​ Growth rates were fastest among applications to the Most Selective
institutions (those with an admit rate lower than 25%) and private
institutions: Applications to Most Selective institutions grew by 14%.
Applications to private institutions increased by 13%, while the number of
applications to public institutions increased by 3%.
8.​ The count of applicants who applied to both in- and out-of-state
institutions grew the fastest: Applicants who applied to both in- and
out-of-state institutions grew by 4%, while applicants who applied to only
out-of-state institutions grew slightly by 1%. By contrast, applicants who
applied to only in-state institutions decreased by 3%.

Overall platform trends
Beginning our review of season-to-date data with overall platform usage trends,
Figures 1–4 display the overall number of accounts created by students intending to
enroll in the following academic year (e.g., 2027–28 for students in the 2026–27
application season), the number of account creators that have submitted at least
one application (“applicants”), the total number of applications submitted, and the
average number of applications submitted per applicant at this point in the season.
Each point in each plot tracks the indicated metric for one season through
November 1, and the final point in each plot is additionally labeled with the percent
growth in that metric between 2025–26 and the current 2026-27 season.
For example, in Figure 1, we see that the number of account creators through
November 1 has decreased from 2,012,437 in 2025–26 to 1,980,780 in 2026–27— a
year over year decrease of 2%. The trajectory of account creators and applicants
has slowed compared to this time last year. Notably, this is the first instance of a
decline in account creators on the Common App platform since 2022–23.2 We
continue to see growth in other platform metrics at this point in the season
compared to prior years, with a 1% increase in applicants, a 6% increase in
applications, and a 5% increase in applications per applicant.

Applicant demographic trends
Though the trends above reveal shifts in the use of Common App over time, the
primary value in these timely updates lies in disaggregating these trends by student
demographics and other key application characteristics.

Trends by student legal sex
Figure 5 displays growth in applicants by legal sex since 2022–23. Female and male
applicants accounted for similar growth rates through the period, with both groups
showing 1% growth compared to this point in 2025-26. Female applicants continue
to account for a larger share of all applicants throughout the period.
Figure 5. Change in domestic first-year applicants by legal sex since 2022–23

Trends by student race/ethnicity
Consistent with results from our previous reports on the diversification of the
Common App applicant pool, we see that the growth in the number of applicants
identifying as URM continues to slightly outpace that of their non-URM peers at 1%
since 2025–26 (versus less than 1% for applicants not identifying as URM), though
the number of these students remains smaller. Note that all plots shown here
regarding student race/ethnicity (Figures 6–8) focus exclusively on domestic
applicants (i.e., excluding citizens of countries besides the United States) in
alignment with federal reporting practices in higher education.

Figure 7 examines applicant growth trends across federal race/ethnicity groupings,
revealing that this growth is fastest for applicants identifying as American Indian or
Alaska Native (9%) or Asian (3%). The share of domestic applicants identifying as
White at this point in the season declined from 50.0% in 2025-26 to 49.6% in
2026-27 (not pictured), a drop that represents the continuation of a long-term
trend dating back to at least the 2013–14 season.3
The share of students reporting Unknown race/ethnicity declined compared to this
point in the season in 2025–26, with 3.3% of students reporting Unknown in
2025–26 and 3.2% in 2026–27 (not pictured). Taken together, these data suggest
that there have been no meaningful deviations from pre-existing trends over the
past decade in race/ethnicity reporting or population growth on the Common App
platform after the U.S. Supreme Court ruling, aligning with our prior research brief
on the subject following the end of the 2024–25 application season.

While Common App does not explicitly collect applicants’ household income
information, we supplement our understanding of the socioeconomic
characteristics of applicants by examining characteristics of the communities in
which they reside from the U.S. Census (for students residing in the United States).
In alignment with broader higher education research practices, our past research
work, and our Next Chapter, we track the number of applicants residing in a ZIP
code with a median household income above or below the national median
household income.6 As shown in Figure 10, applicants from below-median income
ZIP codes increased by 1% while their above-median income peers increased by less
than 1% since this point of the season in 2025–26.

Figure 11 tracks changes in applicant first-generation college student status. For
these purposes, we define a first-generation college student as having parents who
have not obtained a Bachelor’s degree or higher (regardless of when the degree was
received, whether the student lives with adults other than their parents, and
institutional country or type).7
Over the last year, we heard from our member institutions that there were some
applicants who indicated parental education levels mapping to a first-generation
status and also indicated professional parental occupation (e.g. lawyer, doctor). For
the 2026–27 cycle, Common App transitioned parental education questions from a
scrollable dropdown menu to radio buttons to make the full range of parental
education levels more visible to students. Due to these refinements in our user
interface, students appear to be more likely to report higher parental education
levels. This change in data collection and student self-reporting patterns requires a
reset in our year over year comparisons for students classified as first-generation.

Trends in applicants’ test score reporting behaviors
As reported in our 2025-26 End of Season Report, the share of Common App
applicants reporting test scores has shifted dramatically over time. This season has
reversed a multi-year trend of increasing score reporting through November 1, and
instead saw a decrease in score reporters of 2%, with a corresponding increase in
non-reporters of 5%. This season, 5% of members require a test score to submit an
application (not pictured). Because patterns in standardized test reporting continue
to shift across the application season, we will continue to monitor and report these
ongoing findings in future updates.

Trends by member characteristics
We close this report by showing how trends in applications to our domestic
members have changed over time through this point in the season. Figure 21 charts
the number of applications sent at this point in the season to public and private
members, while Figure 22 charts the number of applications sent at this point in the
season to members of varying selectivity bands (as measured by their
undergraduate admit rates reported in the Integrated Postsecondary Education
Data System). Applications to public and private institutions both increased, with a
larger annual increase for private (+13%) than for public (+3%) institutions than at
this time in the 2025-26 application cycle.
Between the 2025–26 and 2026–27 application cycles, application volume grew
fastest among Most Selective institutions (those admitting fewer than 25% of
applicants), rising 14%. In comparison, institutions in other selectivity brackets
experienced more moderate growth. (Members lacking publicly available selectivity
metrics are excluded from Figure 22.)
