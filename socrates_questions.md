# Socrates' Questions and Their Modern Status

The central questions of Plato's thirteen early ("Socratic") dialogues — Euthyphro, Apology, Crito, Charmides, Laches, Lysis, Euthydemus, Hippias Minor, Hippias Major, Ion, Protagoras, Gorgias, Meno — extracted from the public-domain texts, deduplicated across dialogues, and classified by their modern status:

- **ANSWERED** — a mature successor discipline has absorbed the question and its practitioners broadly converge on results. The test is sociological (disciplinary consensus), not a philosopher's favorite answer.
- **OPEN** — still contested; no field has produced convergence. Open questions carry a reason: **(a)** irreducibly normative, **(b)** an empirical field is working on it but hasn't converged, **(c)** other.
- **DISSOLVED** — the question rested on a presupposition we now reject, so it was discarded rather than answered.

Borderline classifications are marked † and discussed below the table. All literature pointers were verified against live sources.

| Can virtue (moral excellence) be taught? | Protagoras, Meno, Euthydemus | OPEN (b) † | Developmental and educational psychology study whether interventions durably change character, with mixed and unconverged results; whether what is thereby changed is 'virtue' remains partly normative. | The question was taken over by developmental and educational psychology as 'character education' research, which runs and meta-analyzes school-based interventions to test whether traits like honesty and self-control can be durably taught, while neo-Aristotelian philosophers of education (e.g. Kristjánsson and the Jubilee Centre) debate what would count as teaching genuine virtue rather than mere behavioral compliance. [Moral Character](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-character/) (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (Marcia Homiak; first published 2003, revised)); [Aristotelian Character Education](https://www.routledge.com/Aristotelian-Character-Education/Kristjansson/p/book/9781138804753) (Kristján Kristjánsson, Routledge, 2015) |
| What is virtue itself — is there a single definable nature common to all the virtues? | Meno | OPEN (a) † | Virtue ethics revived the question and no convergence exists among competing accounts (Aristotelian, sentimentalist, exemplarist); it is irreducibly normative. | Normative philosophy revived the question wholesale in the virtue-ethics movement, where Aristotelian, sentimentalist, and exemplarist accounts of what a virtue is remain in open competition; meanwhile empirical work (personality psychology and the situationist critique) contests whether robust character traits of the kind any such definition presupposes exist at all. | What is courage? | Laches | OPEN (c) | Both a normative question in virtue ethics and a young empirical construct in psychology; neither literature has converged on a definition, though psychology now measures courage as a construct. | Positive and personality psychology turned courage into an empirical construct: researchers elicit lay ('implicit') theories of courage, build taxonomies (physical, moral, psychological courage), and develop measures, converging on a working schema of intentional action toward a worthy goal despite personal risk and fear — while conceding, in explicit echo of the Laches, that no consensus definition yet exists. | Is reflexive knowledge possible — can there be knowledge whose object is knowledge itself (knowing what one knows and does not know)? | Charmides | ANSWERED † | Metacognition research demonstrates that humans monitor and evaluate their own knowledge states, with mature paradigms (feeling-of-knowing, confidence calibration, Dunning-Kruger effects); mathematical logic clarified which forms of self-reference are coherent. | Cognitive psychology and neuroscience took the question over as 'metacognition': since Flavell named the field, mature paradigms (feeling-of-knowing, confidence calibration, signal-detection measures of metacognitive sensitivity) demonstrate that knowledge of one's own knowledge exists, is measurable, and is imperfect in systematic ways — settling the possibility question Critias's refutation left open, while metacognitive neuroscience now maps its mechanisms. | What is piety/holiness — a single essence common to all pious acts? | Euthyphro | DISSOLVED | Rests on the presupposition that 'piety' has a classical (necessary-and-sufficient) definition; modern religious studies treats piety naturalistically as culturally variable practice, and cognitive science of concepts rejects classical definitions for such categories. | No field now seeks a necessary-and-sufficient essence of piety: the cognitive science of concepts (Wittgenstein's family resemblance, Rosch's prototype theory) undermined the classical theory of definition that the Euthyphro's search presupposes, and the cognitive science of religion instead explains piety and religious practice naturalistically as products of ordinary, culturally variable cognitive systems. | What is friendship, and what makes a friend a friend? | Lysis | OPEN (a) | Philosophy of friendship (value, partiality, the 'friendship condition') remains contested; relationship science describes correlates and functions of friendship but does not settle its nature or normative significance. | Analytic moral philosophy holds the definitional question directly — contemporary work on friendship debates the intimacy, shared-agency, and mutual-caring conditions that make a friend a friend, and the tension between friendship's partiality and impartial morality — while relationship science studies friendship's correlates and functions without settling its nature. | Is someone who does wrong knowingly/voluntarily worse than someone who does wrong unwittingly? | Hippias Minor | ANSWERED † | Law (mens rea doctrine) and the moral psychology of blame converge: intent aggravates culpability, and experimental work shows intention is the central input to blame judgments — the reverse of the Socratic paradox, which trades on conflating being able to err with being disposed to. | Two fields took this over and converged on the anti-Socratic verdict that knowing wrongdoing is worse: criminal law's mens rea doctrine grades culpability by mental state (purposeful worse than negligent), theorized in the philosophy of criminal law, while the moral psychology of blame experimentally shows that intentionality and mental-state information are the central inputs to blame judgments. | Can a person act against their own better judgment (akrasia / weakness of will)? | Protagoras | ANSWERED † | Self-control research, addiction science, and behavioral economics (hyperbolic discounting, preference reversals) converge that people knowingly act against their own judged best interest — empirically rejecting the Socratic denial — and provide mechanisms for it. | Philosophy of action kept the conceptual question alive (Davidson's reopening of the Socratic denial and the ensuing debate over how akratic action is possible and whether it is irrational), while psychology, behavioral economics, and addiction science effectively answered the empirical question: hyperbolic discounting and preference-reversal research model exactly how people knowingly act against their own better judgment, and self-control research studies the mechanisms that resist it. | Is pleasure the good — is well-being just pleasant experience? | Protagoras, Gorgias | OPEN (a) | Theories of well-being (hedonism, desire-satisfaction, objective list) remain in open competition in normative philosophy; hedonism is a minority view but not refuted to consensus. | The question survives almost intact as the debate over theories of well-being in normative ethics, where hedonism competes with desire-satisfaction and objective-list theories, with Nozick's experience machine playing the role of Socrates' refutation and philosophers like Feldman and Crisp mounting sophisticated defenses of hedonism. | Is there a rational 'art of measurement' for weighing goods, pleasures, and pains against each other — near against distant? | Protagoras | ANSWERED † | Decision theory is exactly this art — axiomatized expected utility — and behavioral economics (prospect theory, temporal discounting) empirically mapped the 'power of appearance', the systematic distortions Socrates postulated. | Decision theory built exactly the 'art of measurement' Socrates postulated: axiomatized expected utility gives a formal calculus for weighing goods against each other and near against distant, while behavioral economics mapped the 'power of appearance' empirically — prospect theory catalogued the systematic distortions in how prospects appear, and the intertemporal-choice literature showed near rewards are hyperbolically overweighted relative to distant ones. | Is it better to suffer injustice than to commit it — can an unjust person flourish, and can a good person truly be harmed? | Gorgias, Crito, Apology | OPEN (a) | The core is irreducibly normative — no discipline can settle whether moral integrity trumps all other goods; empirical well-being research offers only correlational evidence that prosociality tends to accompany happiness. | The normative core lives on in ethics and the philosophy of well-being as the question whether virtue or moral integrity is necessary for (or partly constitutive of) flourishing — a central axis of the well-being debate from the ancient eudaimonists to modern objective-list theories. | Does punishment reform/improve the wrongdoer? | Gorgias | ANSWERED † | Criminology converges that punishment as such does not reform: imprisonment does not reduce reoffending and severity adds little deterrence, whereas certainty of sanction and structured rehabilitation programs have modest effects. | Criminology and the economics of crime turned Socrates' claim into a measurable question about deterrence and recidivism, and the evidence runs against 'punishment cures': imprisonment and sanction severity do not reduce reoffending (certainty of apprehension matters far more than severity), while structured rehabilitation programs — treatment rather than punishment as such — show consistent modest effects in systematic reviews. | What distinguishes knowledge from true belief? | Meno | OPEN (c) | Epistemology remains unconverged after the post-Gettier programme failed to deliver an accepted analysis; knowledge-first epistemology responds by treating knowledge as unanalyzable — a live methodological dispute rather than a normative or empirical one. | This is the central question of analytic epistemology: Gettier's 1963 counterexamples to the justified-true-belief analysis launched a fifty-year program of proposed analyses (causal, reliabilist, defeasibility, safety, virtue-theoretic), none of which won acceptance, and Williamson's knowledge-first epistemology responds by taking knowledge as primitive and unanalyzable — so the field still actively works Socrates' Meno question, including his own 'tie of the cause' intuition (stability, aptness, connection to fact). | How is it possible to say or believe what is false, or to contradict someone — do the sophistic paradoxes show falsehood is impossible? | Euthydemus | ANSWERED | Modern logic and semantics dissolved the paradoxes completely: the sense/reference distinction, truth-conditional semantics, and formal treatments of ambiguity and fallacy explain exactly how meaningful false statements and genuine contradiction work — a textbook case of a Socratic puzzle absorbed by a mature discipline. | Philosophy of language and formal logic absorbed and dissolved the puzzle: Frege's sense/reference distinction and truth-conditional semantics explain how a sentence can be meaningful yet false (it expresses a graspable sense whose truth-conditions fail to obtain), while modern fallacy theory and argumentation studies give systematic treatments of the equivocations the Euthydemus sophisms trade on. The sophistic paradoxes now serve as textbook exercises in disambiguation rather than live threats. | Do external goods (wealth, health, fortune) benefit us apart from the wisdom to use them well — and is there a master science of happiness? | Euthydemus, Gorgias | OPEN (b) | Well-being science (positive psychology, happiness economics) is actively working on how resources convert into flourishing — income-happiness curves, adaptation, the limits of circumstances — but has famously unstable results and no consensus 'science of happiness'. | An empirical well-being science — positive psychology and happiness economics — now directly studies how external goods convert into flourishing, most visibly in the income-happiness literature, where even a formal adversarial collaboration between Kahneman's and Killingsworth's camps was needed to reconcile whether money's benefits plateau; results remain unstable and moderated by who is measured and how. **OPEN reasons:** (a) irreducibly normative — no empirical result could settle it; (b) an empirical field is actively working on it but has not converged; (c) other — a mixed or methodological blockage, explained in the Why column.

## Borderline cases

Cases where the best-fit bucket was chosen over a live tension:

- **Can virtue (moral excellence) be taught?** (Protagoras, Meno, Euthydemus; classified OPEN): An empirical field (moral development / character education research) actively studies it, but the question also has a normative component about what counts as virtue.
- **What is virtue itself — is there a single definable nature common to all the virtues?** (Meno; classified OPEN): Arguably partly dissolved: cognitive science of concepts rejects the demand for necessary-and-sufficient definitions, though normative virtue theory continues.
- **What is temperance/self-control (sophrosyne), and what good does it do us?** (Charmides; classified OPEN): The empirical self-regulation construct is robustly predictive (a partial ANSWERED), but construct definition and mechanisms are contested (ego-depletion replication crisis; marshmallow-test re-analyses).
- **Is reflexive knowledge possible — can there be knowledge whose object is knowledge itself (knowing what one knows and does not know)?** (Charmides; classified ANSWERED): The empirical possibility is settled (metacognition exists and is measurable); the logical worries about self-reference were resolved separately and partly negatively (limitative theorems).
- **Is what is good/pious good because the gods approve it, or do they approve it because it is good? (Does morality depend on divine will?)** (Euthyphro; classified OPEN): Most metaethicists take the dilemma to refute simple divine command theory — close to a disciplinary consensus — but sophisticated theistic metaethics is still actively defended.
- **What is beauty (the fine, to kalon) — the one property by which all beautiful things are beautiful?** (Hippias Major; classified DISSOLVED): Dissolution is the majority trajectory (no single essence; subjective-response theories), but aesthetic realism still has defenders, and empirical aesthetics studies beauty judgments productively.
- **Is someone who does wrong knowingly/voluntarily worse than someone who does wrong unwittingly?** (Hippias Minor; classified ANSWERED): The paradox itself is diagnosed as an equivocation (skill/capacity vs. disposition to act); the residual philosophical question of the relation between skill and virtue is still discussed.
- **Can a person act against their own better judgment (akrasia / weakness of will)?** (Protagoras; classified ANSWERED): Psychology's verdict (yes, akrasia is real) is convergent, but the correct philosophical description of such actions is still debated.
- **Is there a rational 'art of measurement' for weighing goods, pleasures, and pains against each other — near against distant?** (Protagoras; classified ANSWERED): The formal apparatus converged (expected utility theory), but its normative authority and the commensurability of all values remain philosophically contested.
- **Does punishment reform/improve the wrongdoer?** (Gorgias; classified ANSWERED): The empirical core has converged (punishment severity does not rehabilitate; targeted programs modestly do), but Socrates' claim was partly normative (expiation), which criminology cannot address.
- **How can we learn or inquire into what we do not already know (Meno's paradox) — is learning drawing out something innate?** (Meno; classified ANSWERED): The paradox is resolved (partial/graded knowledge, inductive learning with priors), but the exact division between innate structure and learning — Plato's deeper claim — is still fought over in cognitive science.
- **Is persuasive rhetoric a genuine art grounded in knowledge, or a mere knack — and what is its relation to truth?** (Gorgias; classified ANSWERED): Persuasion became a systematic science — against Socrates' 'mere knack' — but his normative worry (persuasion detached from truth) is precisely what misinformation research now studies.

## Notes on method

- Corpus: Jowett translations from Project Gutenberg (12 dialogues) and a public-domain Wikisource translation of Hippias Major, stripped of boilerplate, in `corpus/`; per-dialogue extractions with in-dialogue outcomes in `extracted/`; the merged machine-readable list is `socrates_questions.json`.
- Near-identical questions were merged across dialogues (e.g. "can virtue be taught?" appears in Protagoras, Meno, and Euthydemus; the definitional "what is X?" questions were kept separate per topic since their modern fates differ).
- A recurring pattern: where a question had an empirical core (whom we befriend, whether akrasia occurs, whether punishment reforms, how learning is possible), a discipline absorbed it and converged; where it was irreducibly normative (how to live, what we owe the state, whether death is a harm), it remains open; where it presupposed classical essence-definitions (piety, beauty), it was dissolved rather than answered.
